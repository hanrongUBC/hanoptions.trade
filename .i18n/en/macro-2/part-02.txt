The cost of a crowded trade
@@ 841326934a html
When everyone knows about a trade and everyone owns it, its biggest risk is often that <strong>there is no next buyer</strong>, even if the logic itself isn't wrong. Once the buying stops, a little bad news is enough to send the price down fast, because everyone wants out through the same door.
@@ 67c4f847b0 html
Clues that a trade is crowded: the media and social platforms discuss it over and over; people around you who don't trade start asking about it; demand for call options in the options market clearly exceeds demand for put options; related ETFs see large, steady inflows; and the stock stops rising on good news. Seeing these signs doesn't mean you must sell right away, but you should stop adding and think it through: when the buying stops, who will take the other side?

In a typical index options market, clients (institutions) buy lots of put options for protection and sell call options for extra income. Market makers stand on the other side: short the puts (negative Gamma) and long the calls (positive Gamma). When prices are high, the calls matter more, and market makers are net long Gamma; when prices fall into the zone where the puts are concentrated, they flip to net short Gamma. The price where it flips is what's commonly called the <strong>Gamma flip</strong>.

Effects around expiration
@@ 8961c112f6 html
