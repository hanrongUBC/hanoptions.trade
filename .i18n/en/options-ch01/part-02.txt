<span class="sn">1.4</span>Intrinsic value and time value
@@ 2c9efcb894 html

@@ a3c58d1f5a html

@@ 974a4351f1 html

A call is in the money when the strike is below the stock price; a put is the opposite.

<span class="sn">1.5</span>Three ways options differ from stock
@@ 9e05d8ef39 html
Options and stock both bet on price, but options have three extra properties: they expire, their P&amp;L is curved, and they come with built-in leverage.
@@ 1654014f46 html
Difference 1: a deadline
@@ 8fb50663b4 html
You can hold a stock indefinitely and wait as long as you like. An option ceases to exist after expiration, so you need to be right not only about direction but also before expiry. The direct cost of a deadline is time decay: a 30-day at-the-money call loses about <span class="num">0.062</span> a day, or $6.2 per contract; with 7 days left it loses <span class="num">0.124</span> a day; on the last day, <span class="num">0.319</span>. If the stock doesn't move, the buyer pays every day and the seller collects every day.
@@ ca28d599e4 html
Difference 2: nonlinearity
@@ 851cc01d81 html
With 100 shares, a $10 rise makes $1,000 and a $10 fall loses $1,000: perfectly symmetric. A 30-day at-the-money call is different: if the stock jumps $10 instantly, the option rises <span class="num">7.30</span> from 3.59; if the stock drops $10 instantly, the option falls only <span class="num">3.12</span>. At expiry it is more extreme: a $10 rise makes 6.41, a $10 fall loses 3.59, and however much further the stock falls, the loss is still 3.59.
@@ ba3893ca4c html
For the buyer, the P&amp;L curve bends upward: gains accelerate as the stock rises, and losses slow down as it falls. The seller has the opposite. This curvature is called convexity, and Gamma in Chapter 4 measures it.

@@ 111ede7d1b html
