You think stock X (now $100, IV 30%) will rise to 105 within a month. To get “maximum leverage,” you buy 10 of the 30-day 105 calls at $1.66 each, $1,662 in total.
@@ f4c8686bac html
Two weeks later the stock is at 104. You open your account: the calls are worth only <span class="num">$1.88</span>, a gain of just 13%. While the stock rose, IV fell from 30% to 26%, and 14 days of time value drained away. On expiration day the stock closes at 104.5, up 4.5% from when you bought. You were completely right on direction, yet your 105 calls are worthless and the whole $1,662 is gone.

Enemy 1: time
@@ 83cd790654 html
An ATM option's daily Theta is about 0.062, or <span class="num">1.74%</span> of the premium. If the stock doesn't move, this option is down to 70% of its value after 15 days. Time decay isn't steady: it speeds up as expiration approaches (Table 7-14 in Section 7.9).
@@ 065ea4682b html
Enemy 2: IV compression
@@ 30a60b55ce html
Vega is about 0.114: every 1-point drop in IV takes 0.114 off the option. The most dangerous case is the IV crush after an event (Chapters 5 and 6). There is also an easily overlooked pattern: for stock indexes and many large-cap stocks, IV tends to fall when prices rise and rise when they fall. Call buyers often find IV dropping just as their view comes true.

Enemy 3: wrong direction, or “not right enough”
@@ 7c87852df5 html
A buyer has to be right not only on direction but on size. The breakeven at expiry is the strike plus the premium. For the 30-day ATM call it is 103.59: the stock has to rise <span class="num">3.59%</span> just to avoid a loss.
