How inflation splits consumer spending
@@ 75b8c6a6f6 html
Inflation hits everyone differently. Lower-income households spend a bigger share of their income on food, rent and gasoline, so they are the most sensitive to rising prices. Higher-income households have a cushion of rising asset values, and their spending power is less affected. By some estimates, the top 10% of households by income account for nearly half of all consumer spending. Economists call this split “K-shaped” consumption.

@@ a8a9c0a172 html

Incentives in an election year
@@ bc39ddb7bd html
Those in power generally want a stable economy and a rising stock market before an election. In the US, the president's party has lost House seats in the midterm elections again and again throughout history, so in the year before a midterm, policymakers tend to have less tolerance for a big market drop.
@@ f9860b32c6 html
This creates what the market calls the “policy put”: when a market drop is caused by policy itself, policymakers have an incentive to adjust the policy to stop the slide. The tariff pause in April 2025 is one example; the violent swings in the Treasury market at the time are widely seen as one of the reasons for the pause.
@@ 49aeb77ba8 html
It only works when the drop is caused by policy. If the drop comes from inflation, earnings or credit problems, policymakers can do little, and a central bank facing high inflation has a hard time stepping in.
@@ e742a6b707 html
You can't know in advance where or when the put will come. People waiting for it are often forced to hit their stop-loss before it arrives.
@@ 7f88b1ff3e html
Repeated policy puts make the market dependent on them, and risk builds up at ever higher levels.

Volatility around elections
@@ e507cdb16d html
The options market prices in elections. In the week or month that contains election day, implied volatility (IV) on index options is usually noticeably higher than before or after. As with data release days, you can use the method from the Macro Trading Framework handbook to work out the expected move for election day alone, then compare it with your own view. Once the result is in, the uncertainty is gone and IV often falls quickly; people who bought protection and didn't get a big move lose money as volatility drops.
@@ a722b70aa3 html
Once the results are in, the sectors most sensitive to policy react first: energy, healthcare, financials, defense, clean energy, and tariff-sensitive importers. The first-day reaction is often overdone, because the market is pricing “the most extreme version of the policy,” while the policy that actually takes effect is usually watered down by Congress and the courts.

Fiscal policy, government bonds and the central bank
@@ 36b05df3fa html
Every government wants lower borrowing costs. The market cares about two things: whether the fiscal deficit keeps widening, and whether the central bank can make decisions independently, based on inflation and employment. When the market worries about either one, the term premium on long-term government bonds rises, gold strengthens and the dollar may weaken. Chapters 3 and 4 of the Macro Trading Framework handbook explain the mechanics in detail.
@@ 4d2d6c1348 html
As an investor, neither question requires you to take a political stance. You only need to watch long-term yields, gold and the dollar together, and see whether the market is pricing in credibility risk.
