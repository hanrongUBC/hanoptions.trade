<span class="sn">Opening</span>Two weeks to go, assigned tonight
@@ bdc33196a1 html
You hold 500 shares of XYZ at $110. For a little extra income, you sold 5 calls with a 100 strike that expire in two weeks. On Thursday evening your broker sends a notice: all 5 have been assigned, and your 500 shares have been sold at $100.
@@ d177e3ee86 html
The options still have two weeks to go, so why would anyone exercise now? Because tomorrow is the ex-dividend date, and the dividend is <span class="num">$1.20</span> per share. If the holder keeps the option, the call will have only <span class="num">0.235</span> of time value left once the stock goes ex-dividend; by exercising tonight and taking the shares, the holder collects the 1.20 dividend tomorrow. That is <span class="num">0.965</span> more per share, or <span class="num">$483</span> across 5 contracts.
@@ cce4aca0ae html
That $483 comes out of your pocket. The $600 in dividends you would have collected is gone, and all you get in return is your $50,000 for the shares two weeks early, plus no longer having to wait for the remaining time value to decay to zero. None of this should be a surprise: if you know the rules, you can work it out before Thursday's close and buy the calls back or roll them to a higher strike in advance.

Physical settlement: the option turns into stock
@@ dd8960859d html
You hold 1 SPY 780 call, and SPY closes at 785 at expiration. The option is exercised automatically and you buy 100 shares of SPY for $78,000. If your account holds only a few thousand dollars, you are immediately short of margin. It works the other way too: if a put you sold is exercised, you must buy 100 shares at the strike; if a call you sold is exercised and you don't own the shares, you become short 100 shares.
@@ ba1148f3b6 html
Cash settlement: only the difference changes hands
@@ ef67309913 html
You hold 1 SPX 7,800 call, and the settlement price at expiration is 7,850. Your account simply receives <span class="num">(7,850 − 7,800) × 100 = $5,000</span> in cash. No shares move in or out, and there is no weekend risk after expiration. What you do need to watch is when the settlement price is set. Standard monthly SPX contracts are AM-settled: the last trading day is Thursday, and the settlement price is calculated from the opening prices of the component stocks on Friday. You have no way to trade around a gap between Thursday's close and Friday's open. SPX weekly and daily contracts (symbol SPXW) are PM-settled and use the closing price on expiration day.

<span class="sn">2.2</span>Expiration cycles
@@ f6da81c0a7 html
Expirations come in three layers: monthly, weekly and daily. An option that is in the money by $0.01 at the close on expiration day is exercised automatically.
@@ 6d8072d3f3 html
<strong>Monthly</strong>: the third Friday of each month. If the exchange is closed that day (for example, on Good Friday), expiration moves up to Thursday. Monthly contracts are listed the longest, and for less liquid stocks, open interest tends to concentrate in the monthlies.
@@ cef93c2f2c html
<strong>Weekly</strong>: most popular stocks and ETFs have contracts expiring every Friday, usually listed a few weeks ahead.
@@ 0fc1576511 html
<strong>Daily</strong>: a handful of products, such as SPX, XSP, SPY and QQQ, have contracts expiring every day from Monday to Friday. Contracts that expire the same day are often called 0DTE (Chapter 10).
@@ b538085993 html

<span class="sn">2.3</span>Exercise and assignment
@@ 344a5935a2 html
Exercise is the buyer's decision; assignment is the result that lands on a seller at random. The seller can't choose it and can't know it in advance.
@@ 1e6a465a05 html
The holder submits an exercise instruction through a broker (or is exercised automatically at expiration).
@@ 859c5311d4 html
Instructions go to the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC). It is the central counterparty for all US-listed options: on every trade, the buyer and the seller each actually settle with the OCC.
@@ 5ca67f6d1c html
The OCC allocates assignments <strong>at random</strong> among the clearing members that hold short positions in that contract.
@@ 978c83744b html
The clearing member (your broker or its clearing firm) then allocates them to specific customer accounts, either at random or first in, first out, and must disclose its method to customers.
@@ bf0fe131da html
The seller is usually notified before the next trading day's open, and the short option in the account becomes a purchase or sale of stock.

Why early exercise usually doesn't pay
@@ 1962b86c0e html
Exercising early captures only the intrinsic value; the time value is thrown away. With the stock at $100, a 30-day 95 call is worth 6.65. Exercising gets you 5.00; selling the option gets you 6.65. That is a difference of <span class="num">1.65</span>, or $165 per contract. So for a holder who wants out, selling the option is almost always better than exercising.
@@ a0282d5867 html
For calls on stocks that pay no dividend, this holds strictly. As Chapter 1 showed, the time value of an in-the-money call always includes at least the “interest on the strike,” which is always greater than zero. Exercising early means paying the strike early and giving up that interest for nothing. Only two situations can overturn this conclusion; the next section covers them.
@@ dea3df31c2 html
Illiquid options are an exception. If the bid is far below theoretical value (say, a call with 5.00 of intrinsic value is bid at only 4.80), selling it costs you. In that case you can consider exercising and selling the stock at the same time, provided you've worked out the costs on both sides.
