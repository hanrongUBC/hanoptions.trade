<span class="sn">5.1</span>Theta: the rent you pay every day
@@ ed6dc37cbb html
Theta is the change in an option’s price for each day that passes, with everything else held constant. Theta is negative for the buyer and positive for the seller. It eats only time value and never touches intrinsic value.

Break down a 30-day ATM call (stock and strike both 100, IV 30%): daily Theta is <span class="num">−0.0624</span>, of which the volatility term is <span class="num">−0.0570</span> and the rate term <span class="num">−0.0054</span>. Buy an option and you get positive Gamma, so you pay Theta. We won’t repeat that here; see Chapter 4, Section 4.6.

Three details that are easy to miss
@@ 7095d1bb2e html
<strong>Theta is an instantaneous rate.</strong> A 30-day ATM call actually loses <span class="num">0.0629</span> over one day, slightly more than the 0.0624 that Theta gives, because Theta itself grows over time. Estimate ten days of decay as <span class="num">0.0624 × 10 = 0.62</span>, and the actual figure is <span class="num">0.68</span>. The closer to expiration, the bigger this underestimate.
@@ e0b88a7b27 html
<strong>The higher the IV, the higher the rent.</strong> The volatility term is proportional to σ. At IV of 15%, 30% and 60%, a 30-day ATM call has Theta of <span class="num">−0.034</span>, <span class="num">−0.062</span> and <span class="num">−0.119</span>, scaling up almost in proportion. A high-IV option isn’t just expensive; its daily rent is higher too.
@@ f0b95ad435 html
<strong>Theta can be positive.</strong> Chapter 3 noted that a deep ITM European-style put gets cheaper the longer its term. Seen another way, its Theta is positive: a 30-day European-style put with the stock at $100 and a strike of 150 has Theta of <span class="num">+0.0164</span>/day. An American-style put would be exercised early, so this doesn’t happen.

<span class="sn">5.2</span>The shape of time decay
@@ d070d98c07 html
An ATM option’s time value decays with √<span class="v">T</span>, falling faster the closer it gets to expiration. An OTM option starts losing big chunks early, and its Theta peaks while expiration is still far away.
@@ 16f13780ba html
The 0.4 rule from Chapter 3 tells us that an ATM call ≈ 0.4 × <span class="v">S</span> × σ × √<span class="v">T</span>. Price is proportional to √<span class="v">T</span>, so daily decay is proportional to 1/√<span class="v">T</span>: cut the time remaining to a quarter and Theta doubles. Theta is <span class="num">−0.038</span> at 90 days, <span class="num">−0.062</span> at 30 days, <span class="num">−0.124</span> at 7 days and <span class="num">−0.319</span> on the last day.
@@ 37e9199afc html
The same relationship gives a mental-math rule: <strong>an ATM option loses about 1 / (2 × days remaining) of its value each day.</strong> Price is proportional to √<span class="v">T</span>, and its relative rate of change with respect to <span class="v">T</span> is 1/(2<span class="v">T</span>). With 30 days left that is about 1/60 ≈ 1.7% a day (Black-Scholes gives 1.74%); with 7 days left, about 7.1% (actual 7.3%); with 365 days left, about 0.14% (actual 0.15%). The actual values run slightly higher because of the rate term.

How to use it
@@ e16577293f html
<strong>Buyers</strong>: try to keep your holding period out of the steepest stretch of Theta. For ATM options, avoid the last two or three weeks; when buying OTM options, pick an expiration somewhat beyond the point where “the strike is one standard deviation away.”
@@ 9b8bcc216d html
<strong>Sellers</strong>: selling ATM options in their last few weeks collects accelerating Theta, but also carries the most Gamma (Chapter 4, Case 1). If you sell OTM options, you actually collect the most Theta between 30 and 60 days out.
@@ 8a79de511c html
<strong>In percentage terms</strong>: in the final week, OTM options lose the largest share of their value each day. With 7 days left, the 105 call loses 24% a day and the ATM call only 7%. A small dollar loss doesn’t mean a small percentage loss.
