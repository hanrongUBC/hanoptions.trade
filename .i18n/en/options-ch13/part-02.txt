The Kelly fraction is a ceiling, not a target. It assumes you know your win rate and payoff exactly, and a buyer’s win-rate estimate is often off by several percentage points. Total exposure and concentration
@@ 0841dcf63a html
<strong>Total stress loss no more than 20% of the account.</strong> A 20% drawdown needs a 25% gain to recover; a 50% drawdown needs a double. 20% is a drawdown you can still trade through normally, without your judgment getting bent out of shape.
@@ 9329ad72c7 html
<strong>Total notional exposure on short puts (strike × 100 × contracts) no more than 1–1.5 times the account.</strong> That is how much stock you may be forced to take on in a market-wide decline.
@@ 1fb929ea62 html
<strong>No single underlying above 25% of total stress loss, no single sector above 40%.</strong> Concentration caps guard against single-stock gaps: one earnings report or one regulatory headline is enough.

Why −20%
@@ ee075cc56b html
For a broad index, −20% is roughly the worst single day in modern history: on October 19, 1987, the S&amp;P 500 fell about 20% in one day. Using it as a weekly stress scenario is conservative enough. For single stocks, a 20%–30% gap after earnings is not rare, so −20% is only a starting point; binary events such as biotech trial results or a failed merger should be tested at −50% or worse. For leveraged ETFs, multiply in the leverage: a 3× long ETF falls about 30% when the index falls 10%. The same goes for IV doubling: for an index that normally trades at 15% IV, doubling in a sharp selloff is no exaggeration; for a wild stock already at 80%, doubling is less realistic, and the price gap is what matters.
