Delta and <span class="g">gamma</span>
@@ 92d211a077 html
Delta tells you how much direction you are betting right now; Gamma tells you how fast that bet will grow or shrink on its own. Read these two numbers and, before you place an order, you can work out “how much I make if it rises, how much I lose if it falls, and what happens if I do nothing.”

<span class="sn">4.1</span>Three ways to read delta
@@ 15af48cd5b html
The same Delta number can be used in three ways. All three are valid, but each has its limits.
@@ d241ec177e html
Reading 1: price sensitivity
@@ 0ad1f3d49c html
When the underlying rises $1, the option's price changes by about Delta dollars. For a call with a Delta of <span class="num">0.53</span>, a move in the stock from 100 to 101 takes the option from about <span class="num">3.59</span> to <span class="num">4.12</span>. Call Deltas lie between 0 and 1; put Deltas lie between −1 and 0.
@@ 999389b824 html
Reading 2: hedge ratio and “share equivalent”
@@ 7cd9df8139 html
Delta × 100 is the number of shares one contract is equivalent to. This is the most practical reading, because it lets you add up stock and different options directly.
@@ fc39270166 html
Direction: +1 for long, −1 for short. Each share of stock always has a Delta of 1.
@@ 3d1bab69fb html
Buy 2 calls with Delta 0.40: <span class="num">+0.40 × 100 × 2 = +80</span> shares
@@ 5db5ae65fc html
Sell 3 puts with Delta −0.30: <span class="num">−0.30 × 100 × 3 × (−1) = +90</span> shares
@@ d8cc57405c html
Total <span class="num">+170</span>: directionally the same as holding 170 shares. Selling a put is bullish, just like buying a call
@@ 2fa2a7da01 html
Reading 3: probability of expiring in the money (approximate)
@@ fafa0516ed html
Delta is often used as the “probability of expiring in the money.” That is only an approximation: in Black-Scholes, Delta is <span class="v">N</span>(<span class="v">d</span><sub>1</sub>), while the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money is <span class="v">N</span>(<span class="v">d</span><sub>2</sub>), and the former is always slightly larger. Also, this is the probability implied by market prices, not the real-world probability.

Time: charm pushes delta toward 0 or 1
@@ f0de4b603f html
Even if nothing else changes, Delta changes as a day passes. This rate of change is called <strong>Charm</strong>. Out-of-the-money Deltas drift toward 0 each day, and in-the-money Deltas drift toward 1. Going from 30 days to 29, the 110 call's Delta drops from <span class="num">0.152</span> to <span class="num">0.148</span>; with 10 days left it is only <span class="num">0.031</span>. The 90 call, meanwhile, rises from <span class="num">0.904</span> to <span class="num">0.985</span> at 10 days.
@@ ab545f94b1 html
Before the Friday close, market makers adjust their hedges on large numbers of options about to expire. Out-of-the-money Deltas collapse to zero and the stock held against them is unwound; that flow comes from Charm. A lot of “unexplained moves on expiration-day afternoons” are related to this.
@@ 1ae1490c6a html
Volatility: vanna changes delta
@@ 1b1ef80b1f html
The change in Delta for each 1-point change in implied volatility is called <strong>Vanna</strong>. The 110 call (30 days) has a Delta of <span class="num">0.152</span> at IV 30%; if IV rises to 31%, it becomes <span class="num">0.161</span>; at 40%, <span class="num">0.228</span>; if IV falls to 20%, only <span class="num">0.057</span>.
@@ 0ecfbb9208 html
This means that when IV collapses after earnings, both the price and the Delta of your out-of-the-money options shrink at the same time: even if you're right on direction, you capture less of the move.
