Two hedging conventions
@@ 70dcda651a html
<strong>Delta at implied volatility</strong> (the most common): the daily mark-to-market P&amp;L is smooth, but the total P&amp;L depends on the path. The $157 vs. $3.4 difference above comes from this convention.

<strong>Delta at your forecast of realized volatility</strong>: if the forecast is right, the total P&amp;L is in theory locked in as the difference between the option's value at the two volatilities. @@ 0093562195 html
Both conventions have the same expectation; they differ in where the risk lands. For an individual trader, a third thing matters more: where your “forecast of realized volatility” comes from. @@ 56fd5629b3 html
The volatility risk premium says that, on average, implied volatility on index options is higher than the volatility realized afterward. Sellers collect this premium systematically, at the cost of occasionally giving a lot back at once in gaps and sharp sell-offs. A volatility arbitrageur doesn't sit permanently on one side: they bet only when the two clearly diverge, and use Delta hedging to take direction out.

@@ 0d5ffd568f html
The less often you hedge, the wider the P&amp;L distribution. The standard deviation of the error is roughly inversely proportional to the square root of the number of hedges: hedge four times as often and the error halves.
@@ 5fbf502074 html
Continuous hedging exists only in textbooks. In the real world you rebalance only every so often, and every move in the stock between two rebalances goes unhedged, leaving an error. The error averages zero, but it can turn an otherwise steady trade into something close to a coin flip.

Three hedging rules used in practice
@@ 4570e168f6 html
<strong>Fixed time</strong>: once a day, at the close. Simple, but it leaves a full day of exposure between hedges.
@@ e724a0f146 html
<strong>Delta threshold</strong>: rebalance only when Delta drifts beyond a set amount (such as ±50 shares, or ±10% of portfolio Delta). Rebalancing happens when the stock has actually moved, so the error is smaller for the same cost.

<strong>Price grid</strong>: rebalance each time the stock moves a fixed amount (such as half a daily standard deviation). It works about as well as the threshold rule and is easier to set up in advance with conditional orders.
