The volatility row deserves a second thought. Once the stock falls below the strike, the most a call can lose is its premium, however far the stock falls; above the strike, it gains as much as the stock rises. So when volatility increases, the call holder just gets more chance of a “big rally,” while the cost of a “big drop” is already capped. The same goes for puts. That is why option prices always rise with volatility.

@@ 5d36ab5555 html
A European put that is deep in the money can actually get cheaper with more time to expiration. With the stock at $100, a European put with a 150 strike is worth <span class="num">49.51</span> with 30 days left and only <span class="num">46.05</span> with 365 days, both below its intrinsic value of 50. The reason is that the best a put can do is pay you $150, and the later that money arrives, the lower its present value. An American put can be exercised at any time, so it is never worth less than 50, which is why US single-stock options don't have this anomaly. But it does explain why deep in-the-money American puts are often exercised early (Chapter 2): the time value of holding on no longer makes up for the interest on getting the cash early.

The one input you can't see: implied volatility
@@ 334dea12e7 html
The first five inputs are facts, but volatility is an estimate of the future that no one can observe directly. In practice you work backward: take the price the option trades at in the market, plug it into the formula and solve for the σ that makes the formula's price equal the market price. That is <strong>implied volatility (IV)</strong>.
@@ f5a1a0d907 html
For example, with the stock at $100, if a 30-day at-the-money call trades at $4.20, solving backward gives an IV of <span class="num">35.3%</span>. That price is higher than the base case (IV 30%, $3.591), and the extra is there because the market expects bigger moves. That's why traders often quote an option as “35 vol” rather than $4.20: IV puts options with different stock prices and expirations on the same yardstick. Chapter 6 covers how to judge whether IV is high or low.
