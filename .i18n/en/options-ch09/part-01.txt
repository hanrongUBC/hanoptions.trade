<span class="sn">9.1</span>What volatility strategies bet on
@@ b5c176408f html
Volatility strategies shrink the effect of direction to very little. The bet that remains is one of two kinds: whether realized or implied volatility turns out larger, or whether implied volatility itself rises or falls.
@@ f1cb5a07b5 html
<strong>Realized vs implied</strong>: if you hold to expiry, or delta-hedge while you hold, your P&amp;L is set by Gamma and Theta (Chapter 4, Section 4.6). The buyer needs realized volatility to be larger; the seller needs it to be smaller.
@@ 0f0f937cbc html
<strong>Changes in implied volatility</strong>: if you close before expiry, P&amp;L is driven mainly by Vega. If IV rises after you buy, you can make money even if the stock hasn’t moved; if IV falls after you sell, the same is true.
@@ 63eee876f8 html
The expected absolute value of a standard normal variable is √(2/π) ≈ 0.798. An ATM straddle is worth |<span class="v">S<sub>T</sub></span> − <span class="v">K</span>| at expiry, so its price is approximately the “average absolute move” (Chapter 3, Section 3.9). For an ATM straddle at 60% IV with 7 days left, the approximation gives 6.65 and Black-Scholes gives 6.63. This mental math is accurate for short-dated ATM options; interest rates and skew add small errors.

When you read this table, look at the signs of Gamma and Theta first: they are always opposite. Positive-Gamma structures (long straddle, long strangle) pay Theta every day; negative-Gamma structures (iron condor, iron butterfly, butterfly, calendar) collect Theta every day. The calendar is the only structure that is “negative Gamma, positive Vega”: it fears a big move in the stock but welcomes a rise in IV, which gives it a special use around earnings (Section 9.4).

Time to expiry decides whether you are betting on Gamma or Vega
@@ ddca788c64 html
Even for the same long ATM straddle, the time to expiry decides what you are mainly betting on. Per $1 of premium, every expiration has the same sensitivity to IV: an ATM straddle’s price is proportional to σ, so each 1-point rise in IV lifts the price by about <span class="num">1 ÷ 30 = 3.3%</span>. Gamma is another story. A 7-day straddle (3.31) carries 0.058 of Gamma per dollar of premium; a 180-day straddle (16.69) carries only 0.0022, a 26-fold difference. So a short-dated straddle is a Gamma bet, a bet that the stock moves a lot over the next few days. A long-dated straddle is a Vega bet: it barely responds to short-term realized volatility, and its P&amp;L depends mainly on changes in IV. The same goes for sellers: selling a short-dated structure sells Gamma, and selling a long-dated structure sells Vega.

Selling straddles and strangles: no floor on either side
@@ 6fffbc3401 html
The seller collects the cost in the table as premium, and the risk flips over symmetrically: on the call side the loss has no upper limit, and on the put side it runs until the stock goes to zero. On a ±10% or ±20% jump the next day (assuming IV rises to 45%), a short 100 straddle comes out at −$569 / −$679 and −$1,321 / −$1,435, and a short 90/110 strangle at −$367 / −$496 and −$975 / −$1,138. The strangle loses somewhat less, but it also collects only one-sixth of the straddle’s premium. The position-sizing rules from Chapter 8 apply here too: size by the gap loss, not by margin.
