1. Use a combo order, not separate legs
@@ 9e1bd41315 html
Placing the two legs as separate orders is called “legging in.” Suppose you first buy the 100 call at 3.70, and before you can sell the 105 call the stock drops $0.50: the 105 call's mid falls from 1.66 to 1.51, and you can only sell at about 1.45. Your total cost becomes <span class="num">3.70 − 1.45 = 2.25</span>, 0.31 more than the combo order's mid, or $31 extra per spread. The stock could just as well move in your favor, but that amounts to placing an extra directional bet with no edge at entry. A combo order also guarantees that both legs fill together, never just one.
@@ a77f86fcf3 html
2. Start the limit at the mid
@@ e6b5f0da41 html
The natural price of 2.10 is the “fill right now” price; the mid of 1.94 is the average of the two sides. Start your order at the mid and give up $0.01–0.05 every minute or two; you will usually fill near the mid. The difference looks small: 0.16 per spread, $160 for 10 spreads. But this spread's maximum profit is only <span class="num">5 − 1.94 = 3.06</span>. Filling at the natural price both in and out gives up 0.32 on the round trip, or <span class="num">10.5%</span> of the maximum profit. Selling strategies make less per trade, so for them the percentage is even higher.
@@ c21409f928 html

3. Avoid illiquid times of day
@@ cc4a2c3020 html

@@ 42c10a6eb2 html

@@ 17827cdbbc html

4. Planned scaling in
@@ d4aa72dfc0 html
Splitting a position into 2–3 tranches that enter on time or price conditions reduces timing error. For example, you plan 3 spreads: 2 today, and 1 more if the underlying pulls back to a support level within 5 trading days without breaking the invalidation price. The scaling conditions must be written down before the first tranche. Deciding after a loss to “buy a little more to bring down the cost” is not scaling in; it is the averaging down to rescue a losing trade that Chapter 15 covers.

A few common practices. Expectancy has error too
@@ abec989d77 html
The expectancy you compute from a journal is a sample estimate: the smaller the sample, the larger the error. Call the standard deviation of individual results σ. The standard error of the average of <span class="v">N</span> trades is σ ÷ √<span class="v">N</span>, and the 95% interval is roughly expectancy ± 1.96 standard errors. If you only know the win rate and the average win and loss, you can approximate with a two-point distribution: σ = (avg win + avg loss) × √(<span class="v">p</span>(1 − <span class="v">p</span>)).
@@ ae28827278 html

For the lower bound of the interval to turn positive you need about <span class="num">(1.96 × 168 ÷ 36)² ≈ 84</span> trades.
