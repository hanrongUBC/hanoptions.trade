<span class="sn">10.1</span>The earnings IV cycle
@@ ecf6f2b992 html
IV climbs all the way into earnings and collapses the moment the report is out. Most of this cycle comes from arithmetic; market sentiment is just a layer on top.
@@ dde3a68c64 html
Split a stock’s variance into two pieces: the <strong>base variance</strong> it has every day, plus one extra <strong>jump variance</strong> on the night of earnings. Any option that expires after earnings has to pay for that jump.
@@ 63392aa18a html
σ<sub>b</sub> is the base volatility; <span class="v">v</span><sub>e</sub> is the variance of the jump (for example, if the market expects a 1-standard-deviation jump of 7%, <span class="v">v</span><sub>e</sub> = 0.07² = 0.0049); <span class="v">T</span> is the time remaining (in years).
@@ ae30d81585 html
The key is the <span class="v">v</span><sub>e</sub> / <span class="v">T</span> term. The size of the jump stays the same, but the closer you get to expiration, the smaller <span class="v">T</span> becomes; the same jump is spread over fewer days, so it translates into a higher annualized IV. That is why front-month IV climbs on its own even if nobody changes their view of the earnings. Once the report is out, the jump has happened, <span class="v">v</span><sub>e</sub> drops out of the formula, and IV falls straight back to σ<sub>b</sub>.
@@ 3e37d5f321 html

<span class="sn">10.2</span>Implied moves and historical moves
@@ 2878d3f040 html
The price of an ATM straddle is roughly the “average absolute move” the market expects, and a 1-standard-deviation move is about 1.25 times that.
@@ d0c1e15b9c html
The derivation takes one line. ATM call and put prices are each about 0.4 × <span class="v">S</span>σ√<span class="v">T</span> (the rule of thumb from Chapter 3), so the two together are about 0.8 × <span class="v">S</span>σ√<span class="v">T</span>. And the mean absolute value of a normal distribution is exactly √(2/π) ≈ 0.798 standard deviations. The two 0.8s are the same number, so:
@@ f0a95d895e html
XYZ from the opening: 0.798 × 200 × 1.00 × √(2/365) = $11.81, which matches the straddle quote. The implied average move is 5.9%, and 1σ is about 7.4%.
@@ 36cc738d72 html
You can also read off a probability that often gets overlooked: under the normal assumption, the chance that the actual move exceeds the straddle price is <span class="num">2 × (1 − N(0.798)) = 42.5%</span>. A straddle buyer makes money at expiry less than half the time, but wins big when winning; the seller is the exact opposite.

Where the ratio thresholds come from
@@ 7371c99a0f html
A common rule of thumb: consider selling only when implied ÷ historical is above 1.2, consider buying only when it is below 0.9, and do nothing in between. The thresholds come from sampling error. The standard deviation of the 8 absolute moves is <span class="num">2.40</span> percentage points, so the standard error of the mean is <span class="num">2.40 ÷ √8 = 0.85</span>. In other words, the “historical mean of 4.28%” could itself be anywhere from 3.4% to 5.1%, which puts the ratio anywhere from 1.15 to 1.72. When the ratio is only 1.1, the “expensive” you see may well be just noise.
@@ aa56dabb8e html
The thresholds don’t apply when the company has just been through a major change (a merger, a new CEO, a turn in the industry cycle), so the last 8 reports no longer represent the future; when one extreme value in the history dominates the mean; or when the options are illiquid and the straddle’s bid–ask spread alone is more than a tenth of its price.

<span class="sn">10.3</span>Isolating event IV
@@ 2c4bc083cd html
The IVs of two expirations are enough to solve for two things: the base volatility of ordinary days, and the size of the jump on earnings night.
@@ a8c7af86e4 html
A straddle’s price mixes in the time value of ordinary days. With 2 days left in the front month, that is not a big problem; with 10 days left, a lot more gets mixed in. The clean approach is to take two expirations that both include earnings and solve the equations from Section 10.1. Total variance <span class="v">w</span> = σ²<span class="v">T</span> is a straight line in <span class="v">T</span>: the slope is the base variance and the intercept is the jump variance. Two points determine a line.
@@ 31ff988c12 html
Subscript 1 is the nearer expiration and 2 the farther one; <span class="v">T</span> is in years. “Event-day IV” is the volatility of that one day taken on its own and annualized, so you can compare it with ordinary days.
