<span class="sn">8.1</span>What selling really is: selling insurance
@@ 4d915dad7a html
Selling options earns two things: time value, and the premium by which implied volatility usually exceeds realized volatility. The cost is negative convexity: as the stock rises you make money more and more slowly, and as it falls you lose money faster and faster.

Premiums, claims and the loss ratio
@@ 2303b712ce html
An insurer's profit is premiums minus claims, and the same is true for an option seller. Sell a 30-day put with a 95 strike and you collect $1.33; at expiry you pay out max(95 − <span class="v">S<sub>T</sub></span>, 0). The expected size of that payout depends on how much the stock actually moves over the next 30 days, not on the IV at which you sold.

<span class="sn">8.2</span>Covered calls
@@ 7bb135942a html
A covered call sells part of the stock's future upside for cash today. On the way down, it gives you only the small cushion of the premium.
@@ 8664f1c07a html
Structure: hold 100 shares and sell one call. If the stock is below the strike at expiry, the call expires worthless and you keep both the shares and the premium; if it is above, your shares are called away at the strike. By put–call parity (Chapter 3), a covered call has exactly the same payoff at expiry as a cash-secured put at the same strike. They are the same risk, and the covered call is not more “conservative.”

Handling assignment
@@ 0e4c652885 html
When the stock rises above the strike, you have three choices.
@@ 1d491b3abf html
<strong>Accept assignment</strong>. You sell at 105; add the 1.66 premium and you have effectively sold at 106.66. If that was a price you were happy to sell at anyway, do nothing.
@@ 8fd33b0799 html
<strong>Roll up and out</strong>. With 5 days left and the stock at 108, the 105 call is worth 3.49, of which only 0.49 is time value. Buy it back and sell a 35-day 110 call for 3.29: you pay a net 0.20 and lift your cap from 105 to 110. Sell a 63-day 110 call for 4.80 instead and you take in a net 1.31, but your shares are locked up for two more months. Rolling isn't free: you are trading more time for a higher cap.
@@ 57b865028d html
<strong>Buy back after being called away</strong>. If you still want to own the stock, buy it back after assignment; that is the second half of the wheel (Section 8.4). The buyback price is usually higher than the price you sold at, and that difference is the real cost of the covered call.
@@ 4c51bdb5af html
Without dividends, American-style calls are rarely exercised early, because the holder would throw away the remaining time value. Only when time value is close to zero (deep ITM, near expiry) or the stock goes ex-dividend the next day does the chance of early assignment rise noticeably.

Selling ITM calls to “lower your cost”: keep the books separate
@@ 1faa342c79 html
Some traders repeatedly sell ITM calls against a stock they hold at a very low cost, and call it “lowering their cost basis.” Break it down: the stock is at 15 and you sell an 18-day 14 call (IV 80%, hypothetical) for 1.62. Of that, 1.00 is intrinsic value and 0.62 is time value. Your cost on paper really does fall by 1.62, but the 1.00 of intrinsic value is just the money for selling your stock at 14 ahead of time. Your real income is only 0.62.
@@ cb3c041124 html
This option's Delta is <span class="num">0.69</span>, so after you sell it only about 30% of your directional exposure is left. What it really amounts to is “cut the position by 70% + collect time value,” which suits someone who was planning to trim near 14 anyway. If you want to keep a full position, selling ITM calls quietly sells most of it.
