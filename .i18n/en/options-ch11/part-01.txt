Trimming cuts upside and downside by the same proportion. Hedging cuts only the downside, and the price is the premium.

Two tax traps
@@ 6e64a3d66a html
“Hedging doesn't trigger taxes” comes with conditions, and US tax law has specific rules for it:
@@ 917f015a56 html
<strong>Constructive sale.</strong> If the hedge removes almost all the risk and return of the shares, as a very tight collar does (buy the 98 put, sell the 102 call), the tax law may treat you as having sold.
@@ bfb42a878e html
<strong>Straddle rules.</strong> The shares and the options hedging them may be treated as a “straddle” position: losses on the hedge leg may have to be deferred, and buying a protective put on stock held less than a year may also affect how the holding period is counted.
@@ 5eba208c8f html
The details of these rules are complex; when real amounts are involved, consult a tax professional. This chapter's approach: keep strikes some distance from the current price, and don't try to “lock everything in.”
@@ 3858113eb8 html
When you should just trim
@@ c11456ac33 html
The reason you bought no longer holds: what you need is to sell, and insurance can't fix a wrong call
@@ e4ce306cb8 html
A single holding is too concentrated (say, 40% of the portfolio): hedging only postpones the problem
@@ 3be2b725a5 html
In a tax-exempt or tax-deferred account: selling has no tax cost, so trimming is cleaner
@@ 5d8a15ffe3 html
You can't afford ongoing premium: protection costing 3% a year will eat half your expected return

<span class="sn">11.2</span>Protective puts: the cost by strike and expiration
@@ 742ea26e71 html
The closer the protection is to the current price and the shorter its term, the more it costs per year: rolling a 30-day at-the-money put every month costs 23% of the portfolio a year.
@@ 68d47b23b8 html
This chapter's skew assumption
@@ 78daf57acc html
In real markets, OTM puts carry higher IV than at-the-money options, OTM calls slightly lower (the skew from Chapter 6), and skew is steeper at short expirations. This chapter approximates that with a simple assumption:
@@ f7860ae9c0 html
On the put side (strikes below the current price), <span class="v">a</span> = 0.6 points: add 0.6 vol points for each 1% OTM (with 91 days as the baseline). On the call side, subtract 0.2 points for each 1% OTM. IV never goes below 8%. √(91/days) makes short-dated skew steeper and long-dated skew flatter. Puts and calls at the same strike use the same IV.

Three takeaways:
@@ c16a8945bb html
<strong>The shorter the term, the higher the annualized cost.</strong> At-the-money protection rolled monthly costs 23.1% a year; buying one year outright costs 5.2%. Time value decays with the square root of time (Chapter 5), so short-dated options lose the most each day.
@@ 4c8e5111c5 html
<strong>Each step further out of the money cuts the cost fast.</strong> At 91 days the at-the-money put costs 3.09%, the 90 just 1.01% and the 80 just 0.34%. The price is a deductible: the 90 put pays only for the part of the drop beyond 10%.
@@ 4506164e6a html
<strong>Annualized costs are only comparable when they protect against the same risk.</strong> The 30-day 80 put annualizes to just 1.0%, cheaper than the one-year put's 1.5%. That doesn't make it a bargain: it pays only on “a 20% drop within a month,” which is far rarer than “a 20% drop within a year.”
