A.1 Basics (24 terms)
TermExplanation
OptionA contract to buy or sell an underlying at an agreed price within an agreed period: the buyer has the right, the seller has the obligation.
Call optionThe right to buy the underlying at the strike price.
Put optionThe right to sell the underlying at the strike price.
UnderlyingThe stock, ETF, index or futures contract an option is written on.
Strike priceThe price at which the contract lets you buy or sell.
Expiration dateThe date the contract expires; US options come with weekly, monthly, quarterly and other expirations.
PremiumThe option's price. It is quoted per share; multiply by 100 for one standard contract.
Contract multiplierOne standard US option covers 100 shares of the underlying.
In the moneyITMWorth something if exercised now: for a call, the stock is above the strike; for a put, below it.
At the moneyATMThe strike is roughly equal to the current stock price.
Out of the moneyOTMAn option that would be worth nothing if exercised now.
Intrinsic valueWhat you would get by exercising right now; never less than zero.
Time valueExtrinsic valueThe part of the premium above intrinsic value; it goes to zero at expiration.
American-style optionCan be exercised on any trading day before expiration; US single-stock and ETF options are American-style.
European-style optionCan be exercised only at expiration; options on broad indexes such as SPX are European-style.
ExerciseThe buyer uses the right, buying or selling the underlying at the strike.
AssignmentThe seller is required to fulfill the contract; sellers of American-style options can be assigned before expiration.
Cash settlementAt expiration the difference is paid in cash and no underlying changes hands, as with SPX options.
Physical settlementAt expiration the underlying itself is delivered, as with single-stock and SPY options.
Option chainThe table of quotes for every expiration and strike on one underlying.
Open interestOIThe total number of contracts not yet closed or exercised; updated once each trading day.
VolumeThe number of contracts traded during the day.
Bid–ask spreadThe gap between the highest bid and the lowest ask; a hidden cost of getting in and out.
Mid priceMidThe average of the bid and the ask; limit orders usually start here.
A.2 Pricing (12 terms)
TermExplanation
Black-Scholes modelA model that prices European options from six inputs: stock price, strike, time to expiration, volatility, interest rate and dividends.
Put–call parityWith no dividends, C − P = S − K·e^(−rT), linking the call and the put at the same strike to the stock.
Risk-neutral pricingPricing that assumes the underlying grows at the risk-free rate and then discounts the expected payoff; the probabilities it produces are the market's pricing, not real-world probabilities.
Theoretical valueThe fair price a model produces; also the center of market makers' quotes.
Implied probabilityThe probability of finishing in the money, backed out from option prices; N(d₂) under Black-Scholes.
Expected moveThe size of move the market implies over a period, usually estimated as S × IV × √(days/365) or from the price of the ATM straddle.
Early exerciseExercising an American option before expiration; most common with deep ITM calls just before an ex-dividend date.
Synthetic positionReplicating one position with a combination of options; for example, a long call plus a short put is roughly the same as owning the stock.
Forward priceThe delivery price: the current price plus the cost of carry. Options on futures are priced off it.
Black-76 modelA variant of Black-Scholes that uses the futures price in place of the stock price; used for options on futures.
Binomial modelA pricing method that works backward step by step through a tree of prices; it can handle early exercise of American options.
Risk-free rateThe short-term Treasury rate used in pricing; this course uses 4% throughout.
A.3 The Greeks (15 terms)
TermExplanation
DeltaDeltaThe change in the option price for each $1 move in the underlying; multiplied by 100, it is one contract's share equivalent.
GammaGammaThe change in Delta for each $1 move in the underlying; positive for long options, negative for short ones.
ThetaThetaThe change in the option price as each day passes; usually negative for buyers and positive for sellers.
VegaVegaThe change in the option price for each 1-point change in implied volatility.
RhoRhoThe change in the option price for each 1% change in interest rates; more noticeable the longer the term.
CharmCharmThe change in Delta as time passes: an OTM option's Delta drifts toward 0 each day, an ITM option's toward 1.
VannaVannaThe change in Delta caused by a change in implied volatility.
VommaVomma (Volga)Vega's sensitivity to volatility; larger for OTM options.
Dollar gammaHow many dollars of stock the change in Delta amounts to when the underlying moves 1%; used to compare across underlyings.
Share equivalentDelta × 100 × contracts × direction; lets you add options and stock together directly.
Beta-weighted deltaConverts each position's Delta into share equivalents of an index (such as SPY), using beta and price.
Delta neutralPortfolio Delta is about zero, so small price moves have little effect.
Gamma scalpingGamma scalpingHolding positive Gamma and repeatedly hedging Delta back to zero, using realized volatility to earn back the Theta cost.
Gamma flipThe estimated price level where market makers' net Gamma turns from positive to negative; below it, their hedging tends to amplify moves.
Discrete hedging errorThe error left by adjusting Delta only at intervals; its standard deviation is roughly inversely proportional to the square root of the number of hedges.
A.4 Volatility (16 terms)
TermExplanation
Realized volatilityThe annualized standard deviation of the underlying's actual returns; also called historical volatility.
Implied volatilityIVThe volatility that makes the model price equal the market price; the market's price for future volatility.
Volatility risk premiumThe amount by which implied volatility on average exceeds the realized volatility that follows; sellers earn it over time and also carry the tail risk.
IV RankIV RankWhere current IV sits between its lowest and highest levels over the past year, on a scale of 0 to 100.
IV percentileThe share of days in the past year when IV was below its current level.
Term structureThe curve traced by ATM IV across expirations.
ContangoBack-month IV above front-month IV; the normal state of a calm market.
BackwardationInversionFront-month IV above back-month IV; common in a panic or ahead of a major event.
SkewDifferences in IV across strikes at the same expiration; for stock indexes, OTM puts are usually more expensive.
Volatility smileIV forms a U shape across strikes, with both wings more expensive than at the money.
Volatility surfaceThe two-dimensional grid of IV by strike × expiration.
25-delta risk reversal25Δ call IV minus 25Δ put IV; measures the direction and steepness of the skew.
Forward volatilityThe implied volatility for a future period, derived by subtracting the total variance of one expiration from that of another.
IV crushThe sharp drop in implied volatility once an event is out and the uncertainty is gone.
VIXVIX IndexCboe's index of 30-day implied volatility, calculated from SPX options.
Implied correlationThe average correlation backed out from the index's IV and the IVs of its components.
A.5 Strategies (21 terms)
TermExplanation
Covered callOwn 100 shares and sell 1 call, giving up upside in exchange for premium.
Cash-secured putSell a put and set aside the full amount of cash, ready to buy the stock at the strike.
Protective putOwn the stock and buy a put: insurance against a fall.
CollarOwn the stock, buy a put and sell a call, using the call premium to pay for the protection.
Vertical spreadBuy one option and sell another with the same expiration and different strikes.
Debit spreadA spread that costs net premium to open; the maximum loss is the net debit.
Credit spreadA spread that collects net premium to open; the maximum loss is the width minus the net credit.
StraddleA call plus a put at the same strike: buying it bets on a big move, selling it bets on no move.
StrangleAn OTM call plus an OTM put at different strikes.
Iron condorA short strangle plus farther-out wings bought to cap the risk.
Iron butterflyA short ATM straddle plus wings bought to cap the risk.
ButterflyA three-strike structure, buy 1, sell 2, buy 1, that bets the stock ends near the middle strike.
Calendar spreadSell the front month and buy the back month at the same strike, earning the front month's faster time decay.
Diagonal spreadSell one option and buy another with a different strike and a different expiration.
Ratio spreadA spread with unequal numbers of long and short contracts, such as buy 1, sell 2; the extra short contracts are unprotected.
Risk reversalSell an OTM put and buy an OTM call (or the reverse): a low-cost way to express direction and trade the skew.
The wheelWheel strategySell puts, take the shares when assigned, then sell covered calls, collecting premium round and round.
LEAPSLEAPSLong-dated options with more than a year to expiration; deep ITM calls are often used in place of owning the stock.
0DTEZero days to expirationOptions that expire the same day; both Gamma and Theta are extremely large.
RollClose the current option and at the same time open one with a different strike or expiration.
Autocallable noteA structured product that ends early and pays a coupon once a knock-out condition is met; the investor has in effect sold a knock-in put. A common variant in China is called a “snowball.”
A.6 Risk and the account (20 terms)
TermExplanation
MarginCollateral the broker requires you to set aside for short positions or borrowing.
Maintenance marginThe minimum margin you must keep while you hold a position.
Margin callThe broker's demand to add funds or reduce positions when the account falls below maintenance margin.
Portfolio marginMargin calculated from stress tests on the whole portfolio; the account requirements are higher, and so is the leverage available.
Buying powerThe amount the account can currently use to open new positions.
Options approval levelThe range of options strategies a broker approves you to trade, based on your experience and assets.
Defined riskA structure whose maximum loss is known when you open it, such as spreads and iron condors.
Undefined riskA structure whose theoretical loss is unlimited or very large, such as naked short calls and short straddles.
Max lossA position's loss in the worst case; the basis for sizing the position.
Stress testRepricing under assumed price and IV scenarios to see how much the portfolio would lose.
Tail riskThe risk of extreme moves that are unlikely but cause very large losses.
Gap riskThe risk that the price jumps sharply between trades (overnight, for example) before you can adjust.
Pin riskThe risk, when the stock is right at the strike at expiration, of not knowing whether you will be exercised or assigned.
Liquidity riskThe risk of not being able to get in or out at a reasonable price because spreads are too wide or trading is thin.
ExpectancyExpected valueWin rate × avg win − loss rate × avg loss; worth trading only if it is positive after costs.
Kelly criterionThe bet size that maximizes long-run growth; in practice traders usually use only a quarter to a half of it.
Maximum drawdownThe largest drop in the account from a peak to a later low.
Trading journalA record of each trade's reasons, execution, result and attribution.
Limit orderAn order that sets the highest price you will pay or the lowest you will accept; the default when trading options.
Multi-leg orderCombo orderFills all the legs of a spread together as a single order, avoiding the risk of only one leg filling.

Notation
  • K₁ < K₂ < K₃ < K₄ are strikes; S₀ is the cost of the stock you bought.
  • D is the net premium paid to open (net debit) and C the net premium received to open (net credit); N is the net cost, positive if paid and negative if received.
  • The View column reads: direction · volatility · whether the passage of time helps you.
  • The two legs of calendars and diagonals expire on different dates, so their max profit and breakeven have to be repriced using the remaining value of the back-month option; see the charts in Section C.

Three reminders for using the table. For any structure with “Unlimited” in the max loss column, you must have a clear stop-loss and a stress test before you trade (Chapter 13). The IV environment is only a leaning, not a reason to open a trade; that reason has to come from your own three signs (Chapter 14). The same structure can flip the signs of its Vega and Theta at different strikes; see the summary table in Chapter 16.

The calendar spread shows P&L at front-month expiry (with 30 days left on the back month, priced at IV 25%). The premiums and the figures under each chart are computed live with Black-Scholes.

P&L at expiry (per share)Profit zoneLoss zoneCurrent price 100

How to read these charts
  • Start with the ends: if the line keeps falling at the right or left end, the loss is unlimited.
  • Then the slope: a slope of +1 on the right is like holding 100 shares, −1 is like being short 100 shares, and 0 means that side is capped.
  • Finally the kinks: every kink is a strike. The more kinks, the more the structure depends on the stock staying within a particular range.

EData sources and tools

Below are only common categories and well-known public tools, with what each is good for. A listing is not a recommendation. Only the main domain is given; check it yourself before use. Fees and features change.

Broker platforms

Orders, real-time option chains, portfolio Greeks, P&L analysis

  • Interactive Brokers interactivebrokers.com
    Broad product coverage, low costs and a full set of options analytics
  • Charles Schwab (thinkorswim platform) schwab.com
    thinkorswim's Analyze tab can draw multi-leg payoff diagrams and run price and IV scenarios
  • Fidelity fidelity.com
    A full-service broker with option chains and strategy analysis
  • tastytrade tastytrade.com
    An options-focused broker whose interface is built around IV Rank and defined-risk strategies

When choosing a broker, compare commissions and fees per contract, the requirements for each options approval level, support for multi-leg and conditional orders, and whether you can see Delta, Theta and Vega at the portfolio level.

Exchanges, clearing and education

Contract specifications, rules, risk disclosures

  • Cboe cboe.com
    Contract specifications for SPX, XSP, VIX and other options; VIX methodology and historical data
  • OCC (Options Clearing Corporation) theocc.com
    The disclosure document “Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options,” which you should read before applying for options approval; volume statistics
  • Options Industry Council (OIC) optionseducation.org
    Free courses on options basics, strategy guides and calculators
  • CME Group cmegroup.com
    Contract specifications for futures and options on futures; the FedWatch tool shows market-implied probabilities for rate decisions

The exchange's published specifications are what count: multiplier, exercise style, settlement method, last trading day.

Option chains and IV data

Rich or cheap, open interest, historical IV

  • Your broker's platform
    Real-time data is most reliable here; most platforms show IV Rank, term structure and skew charts
  • Barchart barchart.com
    Option chains, IV Rank, open interest and unusual volume; some features are free
  • Yahoo Finance finance.yahoo.com
    Free option chains and quotes; the data is delayed, but it's fine for practice reading chains

Data sources define IV differently: which expiration, whether it is interpolated to 30 days, whether it uses mid prices or last trades. Always compare using the same source.

Financial calendars

Primary sources for the event calendar

  • Federal Reserve federalreserve.gov
    FOMC meeting schedule, statements and minutes
  • Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) bls.gov
    Release schedules for CPI, PPI and nonfarm payrolls
  • Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) bea.gov
    Release schedules for the PCE price index and GDP
  • Nasdaq earnings calendar nasdaq.com
    Look up companies reporting earnings by date
  • Company investor relations pages
    The company's official announcement is what counts for earnings dates; third-party calendars may show estimates

Earnings dates often appear first as “estimated” and later as “confirmed.” Use only confirmed dates for event trades.

Macro and company data

Rates, inflation, filings

  • FRED fred.stlouisfed.org
    Macro time series for rates, inflation, employment and more, including VIX history
  • US Treasury treasury.gov
    Treasury yield curve data
  • SEC EDGAR sec.gov
    Official company filings such as the 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K

For macro data, look at “actual − forecast”: markets react most to the surprise.

Payoff diagrams, pricing and backtesting

Simulation before the trade, statistics after it

  • OptionStrat optionstrat.com
    Draws payoff diagrams for multi-leg structures online; you can change the date and IV
  • Broker analysis tools
    Such as thinkorswim's Analyze tab and Interactive Brokers' options analysis tools, which run scenarios directly on your positions
  • Python python.org
    With NumPy and pandas, use this course's Black-Scholes formulas to do your own pricing, stress tests and Monte Carlo simulations
  • QuantLib quantlib.org
    An open-source financial pricing library, good for studying American, barrier and other options once you're more advanced
  • Your own trading journal
    The most important backtest data: only it reflects your real execution, slippage and discipline

Every backtest must deduct slippage and commissions and be checked on out-of-sample data. Treat statistics from fewer than 30 trades as a rough guide only.

Three ground rules for data
  • Use your broker's real-time quotes for order prices; free websites are often delayed 15 minutes or more.
  • The same metric (IV Rank, expected move) can differ from site to site; stick to one source.
  • Any “signal” or “flow” data is an estimate: use it to raise questions, not to replace your judgment.

Premiums and P&L in this appendix are calculated with the Black-Scholes model (4% rate, no dividends, 365-day year). They are for teaching only and are not investment advice. The tools and data sources listed are examples of common categories, not recommendations.
