An intuition for option pricing

The premium is set by six inputs. Five of them are printed on the quote; only volatility has to be supplied by the market. This chapter skips the rigorous derivations and explains what Black-Scholes is calculating, why put–call parity has to hold, and two rules you can work out in your head before you place an order.

OpeningAll at the money, a sevenfold price gap

Three stocks all closed at $100 today. On each one's option chain you find a call with a 100 strike. The quotes are $1.88, $7.01 and $13.75.

Table 3-0 Three at-the-money calls (stock price = strike = 100)
ContractDays leftImplied volatilityCall price% of stock price
A3015%1.881.9%
B3060%7.017.0%
C36530%13.7513.8%

A and B have exactly the same stock price, strike and expiration, yet B costs 3.7 times as much. The difference is entirely in volatility: the market expects B to move four times as much as A over the next month. C has the highest dollar price, but it is buying a whole year of uncertainty; $13.75 on its own tells you nothing about whether it is expensive or cheap.

So an option's dollar price, by itself, doesn't tell you whether it is cheap or expensive. To judge that, you first need to know where the price comes from. This chapter answers three questions: which inputs determine the premium; how those inputs are combined into a price; and how to estimate a roughly reasonable price without opening a calculator.

3.1The six inputs and which way they push

Of the six inputs, the underlying price, the strike, the time to expiration, the interest rate and dividends can all be looked up directly; only volatility has to be backed out of market prices. Start with which way calls and puts move when each input increases.

Table 3-1 Which way option prices move when an input increases
Input increasesCallPutWhy
Underlying price S↑↓The call gets closer to the money, the put farther from it
Strike K↓↑You buy at a higher price, and you also sell at a higher price
Time to expiration T↑↑ (usually)The more time, the farther the stock can go
Volatility σ↑↑Asymmetric payoff: extra favorable moves help, while unfavorable moves are floored by the premium
Risk-free rate r↑↓The present value of the strike shrinks: paying later is better, getting paid later is worse
Dividends q↓↑The stock drops when it goes ex-dividend, and option holders don't get the dividend

The volatility row deserves a second thought. Once the stock falls below the strike, the most a call can lose is its premium, however far the stock falls; above the strike, it gains as much as the stock rises. So when volatility increases, the call holder just gets more chance of a “big rally,” while the cost of a “big drop” is already capped. The same goes for puts. That is why option prices always rise with volatility.

Three observations:

  • The stock price and the strike matter most. The change in the option's price for each $1 move in the stock is Delta (Chapter 4).
  • IV raised the call and the put by exactly the same 0.570. This is no coincidence. Put–call parity (Section 3.5) shows that the call minus the put depends only on the stock price, the strike, the interest rate and time, not on volatility. So when volatility changes, the two have to rise and fall together.
  • Interest rates and dividends have little effect on a 30-day option, just a few cents. For long-dated options of a year or two (LEAPS) you can't ignore them; Chapter 5 works through the numbers.
Exception: more time doesn't always make an option worth more

A European put that is deep in the money can actually get cheaper with more time to expiration. With the stock at $100, a European put with a 150 strike is worth 49.51 with 30 days left and only 46.05 with 365 days, both below its intrinsic value of 50. The reason is that the best a put can do is pay you $150, and the later that money arrives, the lower its present value. An American put can be exercised at any time, so it is never worth less than 50, which is why US single-stock options don't have this anomaly. But it does explain why deep in-the-money American puts are often exercised early (Chapter 2): the time value of holding on no longer makes up for the interest on getting the cash early.

The one input you can't see: implied volatility

The first five inputs are facts, but volatility is an estimate of the future that no one can observe directly. In practice you work backward: take the price the option trades at in the market, plug it into the formula and solve for the σ that makes the formula's price equal the market price. That is implied volatility (IV).

For example, with the stock at $100, if a 30-day at-the-money call trades at $4.20, solving backward gives an IV of 35.3%. That price is higher than the base case (IV 30%, $3.591), and the extra is there because the market expects bigger moves. That's why traders often quote an option as “35 vol” rather than $4.20: IV puts options with different stock prices and expirations on the same yardstick. Chapter 6 covers how to judge whether IV is high or low.

3.2What Black-Scholes calculates

There are two ways to read the Black-Scholes price: as the expected payoff at expiration, discounted; or as the cost of replicating the option with stock and cash. Both readings give the same number.

C = S·N(d1) − Ke−rT·N(d2)
d1 = ln(S/K) + (r + σ2/2)Tσ√T
d2 = d1 − σ√T

N(·) is the cumulative probability of the standard normal distribution. The put price follows directly from parity (Section 3.5), so there's no need to memorize a separate formula.

Reading 1: the expected payoff, discounted

At expiration, a call pays max(ST − K, 0). If you know the distribution of the stock price at expiration, average this payoff and discount it back to today at the interest rate, and you have the option price. The two terms of the formula do exactly that:

  • Second term Ke−rTN(d2): if the option finishes in the money, you pay the strike K. N(d2) is the probability of finishing in the money; multiply by it and discount. In the base case this is 99.672 × 0.498 = 49.646.
  • First term SN(d1): if the option finishes in the money, you get one share of stock. Whenever it finishes in the money, the stock is above 100, averaging about $107.23; weighted by the probability of finishing in the money and discounted, that comes to 100 × 0.532 = 53.237.
  • Subtract one from the other: 53.237 − 49.646 = 3.591. Taking the expectation directly over the lognormal distribution by numerical integration and then discounting also gives 3.591.

The “probability” and “expectation” here come with a condition: they are calculated under the risk-neutral distribution, which assumes the stock's expected return is exactly the risk-free rate of 4%. Why are we allowed to assume that? The second reading explains.

Reading 2: the replicating portfolio

Take the simplest possible example. The stock is at $100, and in one month it can do only one of two things: rise to 110 or fall to 90. A call with a 100 strike then pays 10 or 0.

Now buy 0.5 shares and at the same time borrow $44.85 (repaying 45, with interest, in one month). In one month: if the stock is at 110, the 0.5 shares are worth 55, and after repaying 45 you have 10 left; if it is at 90, the 0.5 shares are worth 45, and after repaying 45 you have 0. In both cases this portfolio pays exactly what the call pays, and it costs 50 − 44.85 = $5.15. So the call is worth $5.15.

Key point: the odds of up or down never appear

The whole calculation never used “the probability that the stock goes up.” Even if you're 90% sure it will rise, the call is still worth only $5.15 in this model. If someone sells it for more, anyone can sell the option, replicate it with 0.5 shares plus borrowing, and lock in a riskless profit. Going the other way, the probability of a rise that makes the “expected payoff, discounted” also equal $5.15 is 0.516. That is the risk-neutral probability: it is backed out of prices and does not predict the real odds of a rise or fall.

Black-Scholes breaks this step into countless tiny steps, readjusting the number of shares at each one to the ratio at that moment. That ratio is N(d1), which is Delta. That is why Delta appears in the price formula and is also the hedge ratio (Chapter 4).

This has an important consequence for trading: an option's price contains no view on direction, only a view on how big the moves will be. Whether you're bullish or bearish is expressed through whether you buy or sell and which strike you choose; whether an option is expensive depends only on whether its implied volatility is high or low compared with the actual volatility you expect.

3.3The distribution of prices at expiration

Black-Scholes assumes that the stock's log returns are normally distributed, so the stock price at expiration follows a right-skewed lognormal distribution: it can fall no lower than 0, and it has no upper limit.

ln(ST / S) ~ N((r − σ2/2)T, σ2T)

The −σ²/2 in the mean is the conversion term between log returns and percentage returns: the higher the volatility, the further the median falls below the mean.

This assumption has three direct consequences:

  1. The median is below the mean. The mean is SerT and the median is Se(r−σ²/2)T. At 30 days and IV 30%, they are 100.33 and 99.96, nearly the same. At one year and IV 60%, they become 104.08 and 86.94: the probability that the stock is lower than today in a year is 59.2%, yet the average is higher than today. High-volatility stocks that “fall most of the time and occasionally soar” have exactly this shape.
  2. The ±1 standard deviation range is asymmetric. At 30 days and IV 30%, the range containing 68% of the probability is [91.72, 108.94]: +8.94 on the upside and −8.28 on the downside.
  3. Symmetric distances, asymmetric probabilities. For the same $10 away from the current price, the probability of finishing above 110 is 13.3%, and the probability of finishing below 90 is 11.1%.

This chart also shows why options are priced in “log” terms. A move from 100 to 200 is +100%, and a move from 100 to 50 is −50%, but on a log scale the two are the same distance (both ln 2). The lognormal distribution handles these “multiplicative” moves naturally and guarantees that the stock price always stays positive.

3.4Limits of the normal assumption

Real stock returns have two features that the lognormal distribution can't describe: extreme moves happen much more often than the model says (fat tails), and big drops tend to be more common and more violent than big rallies (negative skew).

First, see how “optimistic” the normal distribution is about extreme days. Assume daily returns are normally distributed, with 252 trading days a year:

kNormal: daily probabilityNormal: average intervalFat-tail illustration: t distribution (3 degrees of freedom)

4σ0.0032%125 years1.3 years

The t distribution column is only an illustration: it is scaled to the same standard deviation as the normal distribution, so the only difference is how thick the tails are. Tail thickness in real markets changes from period to period, and there is no single fixed number. But the direction is certain: measured against long-run average daily volatility, US stock indexes have had several one-day drops of more than 5 standard deviations over the past few decades. Under a normal distribution, a day like that wouldn't come around even once in ten thousand years.

What this means for option prices

  • The market charges extra for far out-of-the-money puts. Black-Scholes prices every strike with the same σ. If the tails are fatter, far out-of-the-money puts, which protect against big drops, should cost more. The market does exactly that: for the same expiration, out-of-the-money puts usually carry higher implied volatility than at-the-money options. This is called volatility skew (Chapter 6). For example, a 30-day 90 put is worth $0.40 at IV 30%; if the market quotes it at 36%, it is $0.74, more than 80% more expensive.
  • Volatility itself changes. The model assumes σ is constant; in reality, volatility jumps during sharp drops and drifts slowly back down when things are calm. That is exactly the problem Vega deals with in Chapter 5.
  • Stocks gap. Earnings, mergers and regulatory news can make a stock jump past many price levels in a single step. The replication argument in Section 3.2 requires continuous rebalancing, which is impossible across a gap, so option sellers carry more risk in a gap than the model calculates.
How to use Black-Scholes

Treat it as a “quoting language” and a “ruler”: use it to convert prices into IV and then compare IV across options, and use it to calculate sensitivities such as Delta and Gamma. Don't take the probabilities it produces as real-world probabilities, especially in the tails.

3.5Put–call parity

The prices of a call and a put with the same strike and expiration are locked together by one equation: call − put = stock − present value of the strike.

C − P = S − Ke−rT

Applies to European options on stocks that pay no dividend. Ke−rT is the present value of the strike: deposit this much today, and at rate r it grows to exactly K by expiration.

Why it must hold

Compare two portfolios. Portfolio A: buy 1 call and deposit Ke−rT in cash. Portfolio B: buy 1 put and hold 1 share of stock. Here is what each is worth at expiration (strike 100):

Wherever the stock ends up, both portfolios are worth max(ST, K) at expiration. Two portfolios with exactly the same value at expiration must also cost the same today: C + Ke−rT = P + S. Rearrange it and you have the parity formula.

Check it with the base-case numbers: 3.591 − 3.263 = 0.328, 100 − 99.672 = 0.328. Note that the whole derivation never used volatility, a distribution or Black-Scholes. As long as you can freely buy, sell, borrow and lend, parity holds. This is also why the change in IV in Section 3.1 raised the call and the put by the same 0.570.

What happens when parity is violated

Suppose the call is quoted at 3.80 while the put is still 3.263. Sell the call, buy the put and buy the stock (a combination called a conversion): you pay out 100 + 3.263 − 3.80 = 99.463. At expiration, wherever the stock is, you sell it at 100: if it has risen, it is called away through the call; if it has fallen, you sell it with the put. You pay 99.463 today and are certain to get 100 back in a month; after 4% interest costs, you still make an extra $0.21 per share, or $21 per contract. Market makers and arbitrage programs would do this trade immediately, until prices moved back near parity. For individual traders, parity is useful for checking quotes and understanding positions; leave the arbitrage to the market makers.

American options and dividends

  • American options: US single-stock options are American-style and can be exercised early, so parity turns from an equation into a range. With no dividends, S − K ≤ C − P ≤ S − Ke−rT. For 30-day at-the-money options the range is 0 to 0.328, which is very narrow; for 1-year at-the-money options it is 0 to 3.92.
  • With dividends: C − P = S − PV(dividends) − Ke−rT. If a $1 dividend will be paid before expiration, the call is about $1 cheaper relative to the put. This is also why in-the-money calls tend to be exercised early before an ex-dividend date (Chapter 2).
  • Hard-to-borrow stocks: when short selling means paying a borrow fee, puts become more expensive relative to calls by that cost, and parity seems to “break.” In fact, the formula has simply picked up an extra cost term.

3.6Synthetic positions

Rearrange the parity equation and you get four pairs of equivalent positions, each “holding the same risk in a different form.”

Table 3-5 Four rearrangements of parity (same strike, same expiration)
The position you wantEquivalent combinationEquation
Long stockBuy call + sell put + cashS = C − P + Ke⁻ʳᵀ
Short stockSell call + buy put + borrowing−S = P − C − Ke⁻ʳᵀ
Covered callSell put + cashS − C = Ke⁻ʳᵀ − P
Protective putBuy call + cashS + P = C + Ke⁻ʳᵀ

Synthetic long and synthetic short

Buy one 100 call (3.591) and sell one 100 put (3.263), for a net debit of $0.328 per share. This combination has a Delta of 0.532 + 0.468 = 1.000, and the Gammas cancel exactly (a call and a put at the same strike have the same Gamma): its risk is exactly the same as 100 shares of stock, and so is its profit and loss at expiration. The only difference is the money: buying the stock costs $10,000, while the synthetic long costs only $32.8 plus the margin required for the short put. If you deposit the money you save and earn interest on it, the result is identical to buying the stock; if you use it to add more contracts, you are adding leverage.

A synthetic short is the reverse: sell the call and buy the put. It's often used on stocks that are hard or expensive to borrow; the catch is that by then the market has already marked up the puts (see the previous section).

Covered call = short put

The third row is the one most often overlooked. Owning stock and selling a call (a covered call) has exactly the same payoff at expiration as selling a put at the same strike with the full cash set aside (a cash-secured put): above the strike, both earn a fixed maximum gain; below it, both take the loss from the falling stock. Case 1 compares them point by point.

Protective put = long call

Owning stock and buying a put as insurance is equivalent to keeping most of the money in cash and buying just a call. The two have the same maximum loss on the way down and the same gain on the way up. This also shows that the cost of “buying insurance” can be measured directly by the call's time value.

3.7Reading probabilities

Option prices contain the market's view of the distribution at expiration: Delta approximates the probability of finishing in the money, and the probability of touching the strike before expiration is about twice that.

As Chapter 4 covers, Delta is N(d1), and the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money is N(d2). A call's Delta is slightly higher than its probability of finishing in the money. For a put it's the reverse: |Delta| = N(−d1) is slightly lower than the probability of finishing in the money, N(−d2). The table below puts them side by side:

Why the probability of touching is about double

Imagine the stock touches 110 at some moment before expiration. From then on, the remaining paths are about equally likely to go up or down, because drift is small over short periods. So “finishing above 110” is only about half as likely as “having touched 110.” Put the other way, probability of touching ≈ 2 × probability of finishing in the money. In mathematics this idea is called the reflection principle; the exact formula, with a correction for drift, differs from the “double” approximation by less than 0.003 in the table above.

The approximation breaks down in three situations:

  • Larger drift: with very long terms or high interest rates, “equally likely up or down” no longer holds.
  • Closing prices only: the touch probabilities in the table assume continuous monitoring. If you check only once a day at the close (simulated with 30 observation points), the probability of touching 110 falls from 0.266 to about 0.22, and for 105 from 0.57 to about 0.50. Intraday touches that reverse by the close don't count.
  • Real distributions have fat tails and gaps: then both probabilities are underestimated.

Finding “one standard deviation” on the option chain

You'll often hear that “16 Delta is about one standard deviation.” This comes from N(−1) = 0.159 in the standard normal distribution. At 30 days and IV 30%, the strikes one standard deviation away are 100 × e±0.086, or 108.98 and 91.76. At these two strikes the probability of finishing in the money is indeed about 0.16, but the Deltas are 0.179 for the call and −0.140 for the put, because d1 and d2 differ by σ√T. So to find one standard deviation on the chain, look for about 0.18 on the call side and about 0.14 on the put side. The higher the IV and the longer the term, the bigger the gap.

Note: these are probabilities priced by the market

These probabilities are backed out of option prices, so they include the risk-neutral assumption and the premium the market charges for the tails. Over the long run, out-of-the-money index puts have usually been priced above their average payout after the fact, so the probability of a drop implied by options is often higher than how often drops actually happen (the volatility risk premium in Chapter 6). Using them as a reference is fine; treating them as forecasts is a mistake.

In practice

Many covered call sellers choose strikes by Delta: sell calls with a Delta of around 0.2 that expire in one to one and a half months, and buy them back when only about 30% of the premium is left. By the reading in this section, a Delta of 0.2 means less than a 20% chance of finishing in the money, but close to a 40% chance of being touched during the holding period. So decide when you open the trade what you'll do if the stock runs up to the strike (roll, buy back or let the shares be called away), rather than making it up on the spot once it gets touched.

3.8Mental math 1: the expected move

One-standard-deviation expected move ≈ stock price × IV × √(days/365). IV is a full-year standard deviation; scale it by the square root of time to the period you care about.

Expected move ≈ S × σ × √(days365)

σ is the annualized implied volatility. There is about a 68% chance that the stock price at expiration lands within ±1 expected move.

Why the square root? Daily returns are roughly independent, and the variances of independent variables add: the variance over 30 days is 30 times the variance over 1 day, so the standard deviation is √30 times as large. An IV of 30% corresponds to a daily standard deviation of 30% ÷ √365 = 1.57%, and over 30 days it is 1.57% × √30 = 8.6%.

How accurate is the mental math?

Mental math gives a symmetric ±, while the lognormal range is wider on the upside and narrower on the downside, and the whole range also shifts down by −σ²/2. How far apart the two are depends on the size of σ√T:

When not to use it

Before events such as earnings or FDA decisions, IV includes the big move expected on the event day. Spreading it evenly across every day to work out “how much will it move tomorrow” is wrong: before the event, each day moves less than IV suggests, and on the event day it moves much more. Case 3 and Chapter 10 show how to split it out.

3.9Mental math 2: the straddle's 0.8

The price of an at-the-money straddle (buying one call and one put at the same strike) ≈ 0.8 × the one-standard-deviation expected move. The 0.8 comes from the mean absolute value of the normal distribution: √(2/π) ≈ 0.798.

Derivation 1: the average absolute move

At expiration, an at-the-money straddle pays |ST − K|, the absolute value of the stock's move. If the move is roughly normally distributed with standard deviation M = Sσ√T, the average of its absolute value is:

E|Z| = 2∫0∞ z e−z²/2√(2π) dz
= 2√(2π) = √(2/π) ≈ 0.798

So E|ΔS| ≈ 0.798 × M. The straddle's price is the average absolute move (discounted), about 80% of one standard deviation.

Derivation 2: starting from Black-Scholes

At the money, S = K. Ignoring interest, d1 = σ√T/2 and d2 = −σ√T/2, and the call price becomes

C = S[N(σ√T/2) − N(−σ√T/2)]
≈ S · σ√T · 1√(2π) ≈ 0.4 × Sσ√T

The term in brackets is the area under the standard normal density over a band of width σ√T around 0, roughly the width × the peak height of 0.399. The put works the same way, so straddle = call + put ≈ 0.8 × Sσ√T.

Along the way you get an even handier rule: ATM call ≈ 0.4 × stock price × IV × √(days/365). Base case: 0.4 × 100 × 0.30 × 0.287 = 3.44, versus 3.591 from Black-Scholes. The 0.15 gap comes from interest: parity tells us the call is 0.328 more expensive than the put, and the call gets half of that. Add that half back and you get 3.60. The rule also works for backing out IV as in Section 3.1: (4.20 − 0.16) ÷ (0.4 × 100 × 0.287) ≈ 35.2%, against an exact value of 35.3%.

For the terms and IVs you'll use day to day (σ√T < 0.3), the error is under 1%, so you can rely on the mental math.

Two ways to use it in reverse

  • Reading the expected move from the straddle: one standard deviation ≈ straddle ÷ 0.8 = 1.25 × straddle.
  • Backing out IV from the straddle: IV ≈ straddle ÷ (0.8 × stock price × √(days/365)).
Agree on the measure first

Some platforms label the at-the-money straddle price directly as the “expected move.” That number corresponds to the average absolute move, about 80% of one standard deviation. Both usages are common, so before you compare against historical data, make sure both sides use the same measure: the straddle price corresponds to “the average absolute size of the move,” and straddle ÷ 0.8 corresponds to “one standard deviation.”

CasesThree cases

Case 1

Using put–call parity to prove “covered call = short put”

Stock at $100, 30 days, IV 30%. Two people make two trades that look different:

  • Trader A (covered call): buys 100 shares and sells one 105 call, collecting $1.662 per share in premium. Net outlay at the start: 100 − 1.662 = $98.338 per share.
  • Trader B (cash-secured put): sells one 105 put, collecting $6.317 per share in premium, and at the same time puts 105 × e−0.04×30/365 = $104.655 per share into short-term Treasury bills, which grow to exactly 105 at expiration. Net outlay at the start: 104.655 − 6.317 = $98.338 per share.

The two put up exactly the same money at the start, which is precisely the rearranged parity S − C = Ke−rT − P. Now look at expiration, point by point:

  1. Interest on cash: if B's cash sits in the account earning no interest, B makes 105 × (1 − e−rT) = $0.345 per share less than A, or $34.5 per contract. The amount by which the call is “more expensive” than the put is exactly this interest.
  2. Dividends: A collects dividends and B doesn't. But with dividends, calls are cheaper and puts more expensive, and parity already accounts for that.
  3. Early assignment: with American options, A's call may be exercised early before an ex-dividend date, and B's put may be assigned early when it is deep in the money, so the two may be taken out of their positions at different times.
  4. Costs and taxes: A makes two trades (the stock and the option), while B makes only one; the two may also be taxed differently.
Takeaways

“Covered calls are conservative, selling puts is aggressive” is an illusion. A cash-secured put and a covered call carry exactly the same risk. The real danger is using margin to scale up the number of contracts beyond what your cash can secure. That is a leverage problem and has nothing to do with which form you choose.

Case 2

March 2020: the weeks the normal assumption failed

From late February 2020, COVID-19 spread around the world and US stocks fell fast. In mid-March, the S&P 500 repeatedly triggered market-wide circuit breakers with intraday drops of 7%; the one-day drop on March 16 was the largest since 1987; and the VIX set a record closing high.

Measuring these days with a normal distribution leads to absurd conclusions. Suppose the index's daily standard deviation before the crisis was about 1% (about 16% annualized):

The universe is about 14 billion years old, yet days of all these kinds showed up within a few weeks. The problem lies in two places. First, the tails of returns are fatter than normal to begin with (Section 3.4). Second, the standard deviation itself isn't constant. Daily volatility in a crisis is several times what it is in calm times, and measuring a crisis with the σ of a calm period is a mistake in itself.

Now consider what this means for option sellers (the numbers below are a hypothetical scenario). The index is at 100 and IV is 16%. You sell a 30-day 95 put for $0.256 per share, or $25.6 per contract. Black-Scholes says the probability of it finishing in the money is only 12.2%. The next day the index falls 7% and IV jumps to 60%: the put is now worth $7.21, a loss of $695 per contract, 28 times the premium you collected. If IV had stayed at 16%, it would be worth only $2.67. In other words, $4.54 of the $6.95 loss came from the jump in IV, more than 60% of it.

Takeaways
  • Black-Scholes probabilities are calm-market probabilities. Using them to size short option positions badly underestimates tail risk.
  • Risk assessment for sellers should use scenarios: how much do you lose if the market “falls 7% and IV multiplies several times”? Delta and the probability of finishing in the money are only a reference.
  • The market uses volatility skew to mark up far out-of-the-money puts precisely because participants remember days like these. Before you decide that “out-of-the-money puts are overpriced, free premium for the taking,” run the scenario above.
Case 3

A 30-second calculation before earnings

The stock is at $250, and earnings come out this week. The at-the-money straddle expiring in 7 days is quoted at $15 (hypothetical). Without opening any pricing software, what can you read from it?

  1. Average absolute move: 15 ÷ 250 = 6%. The market expects the stock to end up, on average, about 6% away from the current price at expiration.
  2. One standard deviation: 15 ÷ 0.8 = $18.75, or 7.5%. There is about a 68% chance that the price at expiration is between 231 and 269.
  3. Implied volatility: 7.5% ÷ √(7/365) = 7.5% ÷ 0.139 ≈ 54%. Backing it out precisely with Black-Scholes gives 54.3%; the mental math is off by 0.1 points.
  4. Split out earnings day: suppose the stock's normal IV is 35%. The other 6 days contribute a standard deviation of 35% × √(6/365) = 4.49%. Variances add, so the standard deviation of earnings day alone is √(7.52%² − 4.49%²) ≈ 6.0%, which corresponds to an average absolute move of about 0.8 × 6.0% ≈ 4.8%.
  5. Compare with history: compare against the stock's average absolute move on the first day after its past several earnings reports, not the largest move and not the standard deviation. If the historical average is only around 3%, the options are priced rich this time; if it is 7%, they are cheap.
Takeaways

The straddle price, one standard deviation and implied volatility can be converted into one another: straddle ÷ 0.8 gives one standard deviation, and dividing that by √(days/365) and by the stock price gives IV. The most common mistake before an event is mixing measures: comparing the straddle price with a historical standard deviation, or spreading an IV that includes the event evenly across every day.

Self-test: five questions

1With the stock unchanged, IV rises from 30% to 35%, and a 30-day at-the-money call gains $0.57. How much does the put with the same strike and expiration gain? Why?

Also $0.57. The parity equation C − P = S − K·e^{−rT} contains no volatility, so the difference doesn't change when σ changes, and the two must rise and fall together.

2The stock is at $50, a 60-day at-the-money call is $2.10, the rate is 4% and there are no dividends. About how much is the European put at the same strike?

P = C − S + K·e^{−rT} = 2.10 − 50 + 50 × e^{−0.04×60/365} = 2.10 − 50 + 49.67 ≈ $1.77. An American put would be slightly higher, but for an at-the-money option with no dividends the difference is small.

3The stock is at $80, IV is 50%, and there are 45 days to expiration. Roughly how big is the one-standard-deviation expected move? Roughly how much is the at-the-money straddle?

Expected move: 80 × 0.50 × √(45/365) = 40 × 0.351 ≈ $14.0. Straddle: ≈ 0.8 × 14.0 = $11.2. The exact Black-Scholes value is $11.17, an error of about 0.6%.

4A 30-day 110 call has a Delta of 0.152. Roughly what is the probability that it finishes in the money? And the probability that the stock touches 110 during those 30 days?

The probability of finishing in the money is slightly below Delta, about 13% (N(d₂) = 0.133). The probability of touching is about double that, roughly 27%. If touching is judged only on daily closing prices, it is about 22%.

5Someone says “a covered call is safer than a cash-secured put.” Use parity to explain why the two carry the same risk, and name two factors that make them not quite the same in practice.

Rearranging parity gives S − C = K·e^{−rT} − P: owning stock and selling a call has exactly the same starting cost and payoff at expiration as holding cash equal to the present value of the strike and selling a put. Real-world differences: if the cash behind the short put earns no interest, you miss out on the interest on the strike; with American options, early assignment can come at different times; and who gets the dividend, the number of trades and the tax treatment also differ.

Examples in this chapter are calculated with the Black-Scholes model (4% rate, no dividends, 365-day year); the numbers for the t distribution, the historical scenario and the earnings quote are assumptions, as noted in the text. For education only; not investment advice.
