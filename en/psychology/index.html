Trading Psychology

Most people don't lose money because they don't understand the market. They lose because, amid uncertainty and emotion, they can't do what they already know they should. This handbook is about the distance between knowing and doing.

26.6%The chance that a good system, with an expected +0.35R per trade, is still down after 10 trades. After 100 trades, that chance falls to 1%.
    00
    Introduction

    Why trading is a mental game

    Before they open a trading app, most people already know: “use a stop-loss,” “don't oversize,” “don't chase.” The hard part is whether you can still do it at the moment when your loss is growing, everyone else is making money, and you've just lost three trades in a row.

    Trading psychology studies that moment. It doesn't study where the market will go. It studies why, when prices move, you so often do the opposite of your own plan, and how to keep what you do in line with what you know.

    The market is a “wicked” learning environment

    The learning environments we grow up with give clear, prompt and fair feedback: get it right and you gain points, get it wrong and you lose them. The market is completely different.

    AspectSchool and examsMarket
    AnswersThere is a right answerNone, only probabilities
    FeedbackGet it right and you scoreYou can be right and lose, or wrong and win
    TimingYou get your results soon after the examIt takes dozens or hundreds of trades to see your real level
    CorrectionA teacher points out your mistakesNo one tells you, and mistakes are often rewarded
    StakesA gradeReal money, plus emotion

    In this kind of environment, learning by “feel” teaches you the wrong lessons. One lucky oversized trade that makes money makes you more willing to oversize; one correct stop-loss followed by a rebound makes you afraid to take the stop next time. So traders need to deliberately build a way of learning that doesn't depend on any single outcome.

    Three layers: cognition, emotion, behavior

    • Cognition: how you think about probability, risk and outcomes. Wrong beliefs make the right behavior look stupid.
    • Emotion: fear, greed, regret, euphoria. They are not the enemy in themselves; they are information. The problem is letting them place the order.
    • Behavior: what you actually do. The goal of mental training is to keep your behavior reliably inside the plan, even under pressure.

    How to use this handbook

    • Chapters 1–2 build the right way of thinking; Chapters 3–6 deal with emotion, position size, discipline and randomness; Chapters 7–9 cover independence, trade review and the part of you outside trading.
    • Chapters 4 and 6 have simulations with sliders you can drag. Try adjusting them yourself; you'll get more out of it than from reading the conclusions.
    • Chapter 10 is the toolkit: checklists, a loss-of-control protocol and a self-assessment. You can take the self-assessment first and then pick chapters based on the results.
    01
    Chapter 1

    Probabilistic thinking: judge one trade by a hundred

    The outcome of one trade tells you far less than you think. Professional traders treat each trade as one draw from a long series, not as an exam they have to pass.

    First, learn to count in R

    R is the risk you plan to take on a trade. If you buy at $50 with a stop-loss at $47, you're risking $3 per share; that is 1R. A $6 gain counts as +2R, and getting stopped out counts as −1R. Counting in R puts trades at different prices and sizes on the same ruler.

    A system's long-run value is set by its expected value (EV):

    Expected value

    EV (R) = win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss

    For example, with a 45% win rate, an average win of 2R and an average loss of 1R: 0.45 × 2 − 0.55 × 1 = +0.35R. Over time, each trade earns on average an extra 0.35 times the amount at risk. That is quite a good system.

    Small samples lie

    Even with the good +0.35R system above, short-term results swing wildly:

    Table 1-1 Probability that a system with a 45% win rate and a 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio is still down overall after N trades
    TradesChance of being down overallThe wrong conclusion you might draw
    1026.6%“This method doesn't work. Switch.”
    2013.0%“I must be executing it wrong.”
    504.3%Getting closer to the truth
    1001.0%A large enough sample to judge the system

    The reverse is also true: a system with negative EV has a fair chance of making money over its first 10 trades. That is exactly the stage at which many people “confirm” a method that is bound to lose.

    Judge a method on at least a few dozen trades. Judge a trade on its decision process, not on whether it ended up red or green.

    Decision quality and outcome are two different things

    Treating the outcome as the only evidence of whether a decision was good is called “resulting” in decision research. In trading, it is one of the most expensive habits of mind. The chart below is the tool you'll use most often in a trade review:

    Three questions before you place an order

    1. What is most likely to happen? Write down your view and roughly how likely you think it is.
    2. How much can you make if you're right? Express it in R, and say exactly where you'll take profit.
    3. How much are you prepared to lose if you're wrong? Where is the stop-loss, and what fact would make you admit your view is wrong?

    Of the three, the third matters most and is skipped most often. When placing an order, most people think only about the second.

    Being right is not the same as making money

    The same correct call can get you shaken out midway because the position is too big; it can end in “right direction, but the option died first” because the option was too short-dated; or it can catch only the tail end because you got in too late. Your final P&L is the product of four things: judgment, timing, instrument and size. If any one of them is zero, the result is zero.

    Chapter 2

    Cognitive biases: the brain's default settings

    Cognitive biases are not stupidity. They are shortcuts the brain developed to make quick decisions in everyday life. They work well most of the time, but in the market they systematically push you in the wrong direction.

    One of the best-known findings in behavioral finance is that the pain of a loss is roughly twice the pleasure of an equal gain. Many trading mistakes follow from this one fact. Below are the twelve most common biases in trading.

    Loss aversion

    Symptom
    Losing 1,000 hurts far more than gaining 1,000 feels good
    In trading
    You won't take the stop-loss and would rather “wait to get back to even”; you risk a big loss to avoid a small one
    Fix
    Set the stop-loss in stone before you enter; think about losses in R, not dollars

    Disposition effect

    Symptom
    You rush to sell winners and hang on to losers
    In trading
    “Take a little profit and run; when it's losing, hold on.” Your win rate isn't low, yet you lose money overall
    Fix
    Preset take-profit rules (e.g., sell half when it doubles); track your own average win and average loss

    Anchoring to costANCHORING BIAS

    Symptom
    Treating your purchase price as the stock's “true value”
    In trading
    “I'll sell once it's back to my cost”; the market has no idea what your cost was
    Fix
    Ask every day: if I had no position, would I buy at this price?

    Sunk cost

    Symptom
    The more you've already put in, the less willing you are to let go
    In trading
    You keep adding to a losing position, and each add becomes a “rescue” rather than a new decision
    Fix
    At most one planned add to a losing position, and only after a confirming signal

    Confirmation bias

    Symptom
    Seeking only information that supports your view
    In trading
    Once in a position, you read only good news and bullish posts
    Fix
    For every position, write down “the strongest case against”; deliberately read the opposite view

    Overconfidence

    Symptom
    Overestimating how accurate your judgment is
    In trading
    You size up after a few wins; accounts often blow up right after a winning streak
    Fix
    Let risk rules, not confidence, set position size; after a winning streak, size down instead

    Recency bias

    Symptom
    Assuming whatever just happened will keep happening
    In trading
    Three up days and you think it will keep rising; one recent loss and you doubt the whole system
    Fix
    Look at long-term charts and large-sample statistics, not the last few candles

    Hindsight bias

    Symptom
    Feeling afterward that “I knew it all along”
    In trading
    You overestimate your predictive power and underestimate how uncertain things were at the time
    Fix
    Write down your view and your confidence beforehand; compare afterward

    Herding and FOMOHERDING / FOMO

    Symptom
    Everyone else is making money, and being on the sidelines hurts
    In trading
    You chase and buy when emotions run hottest
    Fix
    Take only planned trades; a missed move is missed, and the market opens every day

    Gambler's fallacy

    Symptom
    After a few losses, thinking you're “due for a win”
    In trading
    After several stop-outs in a row, you size up the next trade to win it back
    Fix
    Each trade is independent; in a losing streak, cut size by rule instead of adding (Chapter 6)

    Narrative fallacy

    Symptom
    A good story makes you ignore the numbers and the price
    In trading
    You fall for a theme and keep consoling yourself with the story after the price breaks down
    Fix
    The narrative decides what to research; price and valuation decide when to buy. Write down the facts that would break the narrative

    Survivorship bias

    Symptom
    Seeing only the people who survived
    In trading
    Everyone posting trades on social media is a winner; people who blew up their accounts don't post
    Fix
    Treat someone else's one-off windfall as noise; compare yourself only with your own long-term record

    Biases come in chains

    A single bias does limited damage. Real disasters come from a chain of biases amplifying each other. A typical path:

    1. The position starts losing, and loss aversion keeps you from exiting at your stop;
    2. You start reading only good news (confirmation bias) and tell yourself “I'll get out once it's back to my cost” (anchoring);
    3. It keeps falling, and you average down because you've already put in so much (sunk cost fallacy);
    4. The position keeps getting heavier, and a small loss turns into a big one that damages the account.
    How to break the chain

    Don't count on willpower to see through a bias in the moment. Write the rules while you're calm (stop level, at most one add, a cap on position size) and let them make the decisions for you when things heat up. That is the subject of Chapter 5.

    Chapter 3

    Emotion and state: when your body places the order

    Emotions are not something to eliminate. Fear tells you risk is rising; excitement warns you that you may be overconfident. The problem is never having emotions. It is letting them press the buy or sell button.

    Under stress, the brain narrows

    When your account is losing money fast, your body goes into something like a “fight or flight” stress response: your attention narrows to the price in front of you, and long-term plans and probabilistic thinking get pushed aside. Decisions made then are usually aimed at making the bad feeling stop right away, not at the best outcome. So there is a physiological basis for “the closer you watch the screen, the more mistakes you make.”

    Research has also found that the more often people evaluate their results, the more losses they feel and the more risk-averse they become. Someone who checks their account every five minutes lives through more “losing moments” than someone who checks once a week, even if they hold exactly the same position.

    The six most common out-of-control states

    StateWhat it makes you doWarning signsWhat to do
    FearAfraid to enter as planned; closing as soon as you're slightly aheadChecking the chart again and again without placing the order; sweaty palmsCut the position to a size that no longer scares you
    GreedNot taking profit; adding to chase the rallyStarting to calculate “how much I'd make if it rose another 20%”Follow your preset take-profit rule
    Revenge tradingOpening a new trade right after a loss to win it backAnger; feeling the market “owes” youStop trading for the day
    EuphoriaSizing up and lowering your standards after a run of winsFeeling you've found the holy grailForce a one-day cooldown; halve the size of your next trade
    BoredomDoing something even when there's no good opportunityYour reason for opening a trade is “I haven't traded in a while”Count not trading as a correct decision
    RegretChasing after missing a move, or buying back after selling too earlyChecking the stock you didn't buy, over and overWrite it down, then close that page

    When not to trade

    A simple check that's easy to remember: hungry, angry, lonely, tired. If any one of them is clearly present, make no new decisions. Add three more: short on sleep, have been drinking, or just had a big loss or a big win.

    Caution

    The excitement after a run of profits is also a state that needs cooling down. Many people are wary of their emotions after a loss, yet mistake the high after a win for being “in the zone.” That is exactly where position size most often gets out of control.

    Position size is the best emotional regulator

    If the swings in a trade start to affect your sleep, mood or judgment, the problem usually isn't your mental toughness; the position is too big. Cut it to the point where “I can sleep even if it drops,” and most emotional problems go away on their own. You're calm because your position size lets you be calm, not because you're calm by nature.

    Your position should be small enough that you can make decisions calmly.

    Your body is part of your trading system

    • Sleep: lack of sleep weakens impulse control and risk judgment, the two abilities trading needs most.
    • Exercise: regular exercise lowers your baseline stress level and helps you recover faster from losses.
    • Routine: fixed hours for watching the market and trading lead to fewer mistakes than watching the screen all day. Good opportunities tend to cluster near the open and the close; the rest of the time you can step away from the screen.

    Chapter 4

    Position size is psychology: a positive edge can still go to zero

    Many people think that finding a method that makes money is enough. The math says something else: with the same positive-edge method, if your position is too large, the long-run result is a loss, or even zero.

    Losses and recovery are not symmetric

    A 50% loss takes a 100% gain to get back to even; a 75% loss takes 300%. The deeper the drawdown, the harder the recovery, and it gets harder at an accelerating rate.

    Position size sets the long-run growth rate

    Take the system from Chapter 1 (45% win rate, 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio): what fraction of the account should you risk per trade to get the fastest compound growth over time?

    Mathematically, the optimum in the chart above is called the Kelly criterion. It assumes you know your win rate and reward-to-risk ratio exactly; in reality both are estimates, and they change as the market changes. Bet full Kelly on estimates that are too high and you land straight on the right side of the curve. That is why experienced traders usually risk only 0.5% to 2% of the account per trade.

    Same system, different sizes, different lives

    Turn “how big a position” into an arithmetic problem

    First decide what percentage of the account you're willing to lose per trade, then work back from the stop-loss distance to the number of shares. That way your position size no longer depends on your mood at the time.

    Gut check

    A more intuitive test: if this trade hits its stop, would the money lost spoil the rest of your day? If so, the position is too big, whatever the arithmetic says.

    Chapter 5

    Discipline and rules: make the decision before you need it

    Discipline is not gritting your teeth under pressure. It is making your decisions while you're calm, so that when the pressure comes, all that's left is execution.

    Why decide in advance

    Calm and agitated, you are almost two different decision-makers. When you're calm, you understand perfectly well what a stop-loss is for; as the loss grows, you'll find a hundred reasons to wait a little longer. Psychology has a very practical tool called the “if… then…” plan: tying a situation to an action ahead of time works far better than simply resolving to do better.

    • If it closes below $47, sell at the next day's open, without reading the news.
    • If the day's loss reaches 3% of the account, close the trading app until tomorrow.
    • If I get stopped out three times in a row, halve the size of the next trade.

    What a trading plan must spell out, at a minimum

    ItemQuestion to answer
    ThesisWhy do I think it will move this way? Spell it out in a sentence or two
    Entry conditionsWhat price or signal has to appear before I get in?
    Invalidation criteriaWhat fact would show I'm wrong? (Not just price)
    Stop-lossWhere I admit I'm wrong, and how many R I lose
    Take profit and exitWhere I sell part of it, and what I do with the rest
    Position sizeShares or contracts, worked out with the Chapter 4 method
    Time stopHow long it can go without moving as expected before I exit
    Conditions for addingWhen I add, and at most how many times

    Stop-losses are a cost of doing business

    A restaurant pays rent; a trader pays stop-losses. Taking a stop isn't admitting you're stupid. It means the situation has changed and your view no longer holds, so you preserve your strength for the next opportunity. Think of stops as a cost rather than a failure, and they become much easier to execute.

    It is just as important to tell two things apart: price swings and a broken thesis. While the thesis still holds, you can ride out normal swings; that is waiting for confirmation. Once the facts have overturned the thesis, waiting longer is just stubbornly holding on. Write the difference into your invalidation criteria before you place the order.

    Trade less, raise the bar

    The more often you trade, the more commissions, slippage and emotional mistakes pile up. For most people, the fastest way to improve trade quality is not to win more but to make fewer mistakes: cut the trades that aren't in your plan and raise the bar. When there's no good opportunity, being in cash is itself a position.

    One way to measure it

    Sort your trades into two groups: those executed according to plan, and those with clear violations (chasing, no stop, oversized). Your confidence should come from a rising share of the first group, not from how much you've made lately.

    On breaking the rules

    Rules are not right forever; market conditions change. But rule changes belong in your weekend trade review, not in an impulse during trading hours. If you really must make an exception during the session, three conditions apply: very high conviction, a separate record afterward, and it must not become a habit.

    Chapter 6

    Streaks: randomness runs longer than you think

    People misjudge randomness in a systematic way: we expect good and bad luck to alternate quickly, but runs in a random sequence are much longer than intuition suggests.

    A losing streak is almost certain to come

    Over 100 trades, a system with a 50% win rate has an 81% chance of at least one run of 5 straight losses, and better-than-even odds of a 6-trade losing streak. That doesn't mean the system is broken; it is simply what coin flips look like.

    Table 6-1 Probability of at least one run of k straight losses in 100 trades
    Win rate4 in a row5 in a row6 in a row8 in a row10 in a row
    40%99.9%97.6%87.3%49.0%20.5%
    50%97.3%81.0%54.6%17.0%4.4%
    60%80.1%45.9%21.2%3.6%0.6%

    What to do in a losing streak

    1. First, classify it: is this streak within your system's “normal range”? Use the chart above to judge.
    2. Then check execution: were the losing trades taken according to plan, or were there violations? A losing streak with violations is a discipline problem, not a luck problem.
    3. Slow down by rule: set drawdown rules in advance. For example, halve your size at a 6% drawdown; at a 10% drawdown, stop trading for a week and do a full trade review.
    4. Never size up to win it back: the gambler's fallacy will tell you you're “due for a win,” but the next trade's win rate doesn't go up because you lost the previous ones.

    Be even more careful in a winning streak

    Accounts rarely blow up during a losing streak; it happens more often after a run of wins. Confidence swells, size grows, standards slip, and then one big loss gives back weeks of profits along with capital. A winning streak is also part of a random sequence; it doesn't mean you've suddenly gotten good.

    Let profits make your account bigger, not your nerve.

    • After a big win, take a day off, then return to your normal rhythm.
    • During a winning streak, keep your usual risk per trade; don't raise it because you're “in the zone.”
    • Review the trades from a winning streak separately, and find the parts that came down to luck.

    Chapter 7

    Independent judgment: information, narratives and copy trading

    More information doesn't necessarily mean better judgment. What decides the outcome is how you process information, and who is ultimately responsible for the decision.

    Price often moves before the news

    Often the price moves first, and the news that explains the move comes later. A more useful question when you read the news: did the market react to it the way you expected? Good news that doesn't lift the price, or bad news that doesn't sink it, is a signal in itself. When you can't make sense of a situation, asking “who benefits?” often helps more than reading ten analyses.

    Your information diet

    • Many sources, many viewpoints: read two accounts of the same event from opposite sides, and compare what each one emphasizes and what each one avoids.
    • Separate signal from noise: for someone who trades on a daily or weekly timeframe, most intraday news is noise.
    • Don't let your views trade for you: which party you support or which company you like has nothing to do with where the price goes.
    • Be wary of posted wins: screenshots of huge profits on social media are a filtered sample. See enough of them and you'll grow dissatisfied with your own normal returns, and then take on more risk.

    The real cost of copy trading

    Following someone else's trades looks effortless, but in fact you copy only the easiest part of the trade:

    What you can copyWhat you can't copy
    What to buy, and roughly at what priceWhy they bought, and when they'd admit they're wrong
    The moment they open the positionThe later trims, stop-losses, hedges and changes of mind
    The tickerTheir position size, account size and risk tolerance
    Their confidenceYour own confidence (conviction can't be borrowed)

    So copy traders often run into this: the other trader has stopped out while you're still holding; they test with a small position while you bet big; they had a hedge leg you didn't copy. Same trade: they take a small loss, you take a big one.

    Before you act on someone else's idea
    1. Can I explain the logic of this trade in my own words?
    2. Do I know under what conditions it stops working?
    3. Is this position size right for my account and my nerves?
    4. If they stopped posting updates tomorrow, would I know how to manage this position?

    If you can't answer any one of the four, don't take the trade yet.

    Independent doesn't mean contrarian

    Going against the crowd is not an edge in itself. Independence means you take responsibility for every decision you make: you can explain it, and you can admit when it's wrong. You can learn ways of thinking from anyone, but you are the one who presses the button, and you are the one who lives with the result. What you learn from others should be their thinking process and methods, not their conclusions.

    Review: turning losses into tuition

    A trader who doesn't review is taking exam after exam; a trader who reviews is studying a notebook of wrong answers. A loss is tuition only once you understand it; otherwise it's a tax.

    What to record in a trading journal

    Recording only P&L is the slowest way to improve. Your journal should record your mind, not your account.

    Date / ticker / direction / instrument:
Reason for entry (thesis):
Confidence at the time (1–5) and emotions:
Plan: entry ___ stop ___ target ___ size ___R
Actual: entry ___ exit ___ result ___R
What I got right:
What I got wrong:
Did I break a rule? Which one:
If I did it again, I would:
Category: judgment error / execution error / risk-control error / normal variance

    Attributing losses: four drawers

    Put every loss into one of the four drawers below. Put it in the wrong drawer and you'll fix the wrong thing.

    DrawerTypical examplesHow to fix it
    Judgment errorThe thesis itself didn't hold; you misread what was driving the moveImprove your analysis; fill gaps in your knowledge
    Execution errorChased the entry; didn't take the stop-loss as planned; changed the script midwayTighten your rules and your “if… then…” plans
    Risk-control errorPosition so big you were forced to close at the worst point; instrument too short-datedGo back to Chapter 4 and redo your sizing
    Normal varianceExecuted as planned; the odds went against youChange nothing; it's a cost
    The easiest one to miss

    Winning trades need to be classified too. Chasing an entry, no stop set, oversized: even if these trades made money, they belong in “Execution error” or “Risk-control error.” If they aren't corrected, sooner or later they will be paid back with a bigger loss.

    Review weekly, tally monthly

    • Weekly: read through this week's journal; find the single biggest mistake; pick just one behavior to improve next week. Changing one thing at a time works better than changing five at once.
    • Monthly: tally your win rate, average win (R), average loss (R), expected value (EV), maximum drawdown and the number of trades that broke the rules.
    • One especially useful number: add up the P&L of all rule-breaking trades separately. Many people find that without those trades, the whole month would have been profitable.

    Real progress rarely comes from finding better entries. It comes mostly from skipping the few trades you shouldn't have taken.

    Chapter 9

    Temperament and life: the part outside trading

    The market works like an amplifier: impulsiveness, procrastination, vanity and an inability to say no in your life all get amplified into numbers in your account. So the further you go in trading, the more of the work happens outside trading.

    Habits from your life follow you into the market

    • Grabbing at small gains: skipping protection to save a little on fees, not taking profit to squeeze out a bit more, and ending up trapped in positions that get harder and harder to exit.
    • No boundaries: people who can't say no to others in life also find it hard to say “no” to a losing position in the market; one “just wait a bit longer” from someone else and they waver.
    • The “close enough” mindset: on things that matter, “close enough” usually means giving up your standards. A stop-loss that has “more or less” been hit, a position size that is “more or less” right: these are all holes.

    How much money you need decides how patient you can be

    The higher your living costs and the more you depend on trading income, the more urgently you demand things from the market, and the more likely you are to force trades to make money. Conversely, with low living costs you ask little of the market and can wait for good opportunities. What financial freedom buys you is not so much money as choice: you can wait, you can sit out, and you don't have to prove yourself to anyone.

    Especially for students and beginners

    Trading with living expenses, tuition or borrowed money loads the heaviest psychological pressure straight into every trade. The smaller your capital, the less you should trade and the more slowly you should build skill. First learn not to lose big; then think about making money fast.

    The long view

    Trading is more like a professional skill that takes years of training than a chance to get rich overnight. A reasonable expectation: spend a few years surviving, building a method and accumulating a sample, then let a small, steady edge slowly compound. People who aim for “small, steady gains every week” usually end up going further than people who aim to “double it in one shot.”

    Take full responsibility for results

    Blaming losses on the big players, the news, a teacher or luck costs you the chance to improve. Taking responsibility for every decision isn't blaming yourself; it is taking back control. Only you can decide how to make the next trade.

    Don't stake your self-worth on your P&L

    If a loss makes you feel like a failure and a win makes you feel brilliant, your emotions will be led around by your account. Focus on “did I follow my plan today?” rather than “how much did I make today?” The first is entirely in your control; the second isn't.
