    Introduction

    Where this list came from

    On September 30, a trader posted 17 “short-term oversold” stocks in the group chat. His reasoning: September's losers had been sold too hard, and once rates and oil came down, money would rotate back into them out of chips and AI stocks. This handbook takes apart every company on the list, plus the 3 others he actually bought in early October, one at a time.

    His original thesis

    • The rotation thesis: “Buy September's losers on the pullback, short the winners on the bounce.” Chips and AI infrastructure, which led in September, could pull back, while the consumer, financial, utility and industrial stocks that were sold off could rebound.
    • Macro premises: crude oil falls back below $90; yields come down and rate-hike expectations cool; the equal-weight index (RSP) and small caps (IWM) keep outperforming the Nasdaq 100 (QQQ).
    • Instruments and time frame: buy the stock, or buy call debit spreads when volume is high enough—“try not to go naked.” Hold for about a month and aim for a 3% to 8% gain, with expiration at the end of October or on the November options expiration (OpEx) date. On October 6 he added that he had moved into a “buy stock, not options” phase, because the thesis needs time to play out.
    • Discipline: enter in the last 30 minutes of the session; add only after confirmation; set a stop-loss on every stock.

    Chapter 1

    Method: three angles on each company

    Fundamentals tell you what the company is worth, news tells you why the market is repricing it now, and technicals tell you where buyers and sellers stand at this moment. An oversold rebound trade needs all three.

    Fundamentals: the business first, then the price

    MetricThe question it answersHow to read it
    Revenue growthWhether the business is growing or shrinkingLook at both the latest quarter and the trailing 12 months; the direction matters more than any single number
    Operating margin, net marginHow much of every $100 of revenue the company keepsCompare with peers and with the company's own history; margins often start falling before revenue does
    Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E), trailing 12 months / forwardWhat the market pays for $1 of earningsForward P/E uses analysts' forecasts for the next 12 months; when earnings estimates are cut, it “gets more expensive”
    PEGWhether valuation matches growthP/E divided by the earnings growth rate; lower is cheaper, but the growth rate is a forecast and can be far off
    EV/EBITDAValuation that counts debt tooFor heavily indebted companies (such as MGM Resorts), looking only at P/E understates the risk
    Dividend yield, payout ratioHow much cash comes back to you, and whether it can lastA high dividend with a very high payout ratio (near or above 100%) can be a warning sign of a dividend cut
    Total debt, free cash flow (FCF)How well the company can absorb shocksWhen rates rise, heavily indebted companies come under pressure on both valuation and profits

    Banks are the exception. A bank's “debt” is deposits and borrowings, its raw material, so you can't compare it with an ordinary company's. For banks, look at net interest margin (NIM), price-to-book ratio (P/B), ROE and capital adequacy.

    News: find out why the stock fell

    When a stock falls 20%, there is always a reason: results that missed expectations, lowered guidance, management changes, lawsuits, weakening industry data, or simply money pulling out of the whole sector. It is very important to tell two situations apart:

    Dragged down by the sector (more likely to rebound)
    • No bad news at the company itself
    • Peers fell too
    • The cause is macro or sector rotation
    • If macro conditions improve, the stock has room to recover
    A problem at the company itself (harder to rebound)
    • Falling results, lowered guidance, a dividend cut
    • A wave of analyst price-target cuts
    • Takeover hopes fade, major shareholders exit
    • Needs new good news to turn around

    Also list the catalysts ahead. Oversold stocks are most prone to big moves around earnings: if the report shows the problem is not as bad as feared, the stock can recover quickly; if it confirms the worries, the stock can drop another leg.

    Technicals: price, moving averages, RSI and key levels

    ToolHow to read itHow to use it in an oversold rebound
    50-day and 200-day moving averagesPrice above a moving average leans strong, below it leans weak; the 50-day below the 200-day is called “bearish alignment”The first confirmation of a rebound is reclaiming the 50-day moving average
    RSI (14-day)Runs from 0 to 100; below 30 is oversold, above 70 is overboughtOversold means it fell fast, not that it has hit bottom; RSI can stay below 30 for a long time
    52-week rangeHow far the price is from its one-year high and lowNear the 52-week low, holding that low is the first test
    Support and resistanceLevels where the price has repeatedly stopped falling or stalledA break below support means you were wrong; a rebound starts only when resistance breaks
    GapA price range with no trading between two sessions, often caused by newsA gap caused by bad news often becomes resistance later

    Putting the three together

    CombinationWhat it meansCommon approach
    Solid fundamentals, news is a sector drag, technically oversoldThe textbook oversold rebound candidateWait for signs it has stopped falling; build small positions in stages
    Weakening fundamentals, company-specific bad news, technically oversoldMay be “catching a falling knife”Wait for earnings or new news to show the worst is over
    Solid fundamentals, price has already reclaimed the moving averagesThe rebound has already startedYou miss the bottom, but with more certainty
    Very low valuation, very high dividend yield, but falling earningsMay be a value trapFirst check whether the payout ratio and free cash flow can support the dividend

    Oversold means it was sold too fast; cheap means the price is low. Neither means it is about to rise.

    Exercises

    01MGM Resorts has an RSI of just 14, the lowest of all the companies. Is it the best rebound opportunity?

    An RSI of 14 means the selling has been extremely concentrated, but the cause is specific: the major shareholder withdrew its $48.30-per-share buyout offer, the takeover premium vanished in a single day, and Las Vegas visitor numbers and Macau gaming revenue are weakening too. This is a “company-specific problem,” and an oversold stock can stay oversold.

    The safer approach is to wait for confirmation: the 52-week low holds, the stock reclaims the gap level, or an earnings report shows the Las Vegas business has not gotten worse.

    02SLB is also on this “falling rates and oil prices” rebound list. Does that make sense?

    The directions conflict. Falling oil prices help airlines, cruise lines and trucking, but usually hurt oilfield services companies. SLB seems to have made the list because it fell a lot on its own and is not expensive, not because of the rotation logic.

    When you study a list, check whether each stock really fits the main theme, and evaluate the ones that don't on their own.

    03Comcast trades at a forward P/E below 6 with a dividend yield above 6%. Why is it still falling when it is this cheap?

    Cheap usually has a reason: broadband subscribers are leaving, earnings are falling, analysts are downgrading the stock, and the company is in the middle of a spin-off. The market is worried about future profits, not past ones.

    To judge a stock like this, look at whether the payout ratio and free cash flow can support the dividend, and whether subscriber losses show signs of bottoming out.

    04Bank of America reports earnings on October 14. Which numbers should you watch most?

    Investment banking fees (management has already said they will be lower than last year); net interest income (NII) and net interest margin (NIM) (the effect of a flatter yield curve); and bad debts on credit cards and consumer loans (how healthy consumers are).

    It is also the opening shot of bank earnings season, and the market will use it to judge the state of the financial sector and of consumers.
